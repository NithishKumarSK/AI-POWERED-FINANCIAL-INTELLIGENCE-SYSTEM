# Every ceiling-breaking claim has a seam

Every paper examined in this deep dive that reports stock-direction accuracy above the established 55-66% academic ceiling turns out, under full-methodology scrutiny, to be measuring something other than what its headline number implies — a mechanically easier target (a 5-day moving average instead of raw price), an identical-dataset architectural add-on the authors themselves call "partly circular," an instant-reaction window before public release rather than a tradable forecast, or a result that the authors quietly reversed in a later revision. Of eight research efforts probed to the level of named authors, full methodology, and every disclosed number — Zhong & Hitchcock, the University of Ottawa's Al Ridhawi/Haj Ali/Al Osman group (two papers), Lopez-Lira & Tang, the PLOS Complex Systems SPY paper, TradingAgents, CUHK's Lookahead Propensity detector, MemGuard-Alpha, Chen & Pu's agentic nowcaster, and the Selection Bottleneck judge-vs-vote study — not one survives as an unambiguous, generalizable exception to the ceiling once its leakage controls, test-window selection, and self-disclosed caveats are read in full. What does survive, cleanly, is a single confirmed mechanism finding from a non-finance study: judge-based selection among diverse model outputs beats majority voting by a huge, statistically ironclad margin (Bradley-Terry win rate 0.810 vs. 0.496), while majority voting itself is statistically indistinguishable from a single model (Δ = -0.016, p = 0.669) ([arXiv 2603.20324](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.20324)). For a Gemini-based three-call consensus engine already running majority vote at ~54% direction accuracy, that one number is worth more engineering attention than every accuracy-ceiling-breaking headline combined — and the deep dive's clearest secondary lesson is that debate theater (TradingAgents' Bull/Bear/risk-committee architecture) and naive memorization filters (MemGuard-Alpha) are far weaker bets than they first appeared.

## The two highest published numbers both hide a narrow, almost circular foundation

Shan Zhong and David B. Hitchcock's 66.18%-index / 62.09%-stock result is the most credible of the ceiling-adjacent papers precisely because it is the least flashy: Zhong was a University of South Carolina Statistics PhD student (now a machine learning engineer; her dissertation covered "deep learning, clustering, and decision process approaches for time series") working under Hitchcock, a tenured statistics professor whose broader career is in functional data analysis and environmental statistics, not finance — this was a one-off excursion, not an established finance-ML lab's flagship result ([arXiv 2108.10826](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.10826)). It was published in a real, Scopus-indexed journal (*Statistics, Optimization and Information Computing*, CiteScore 2.1) after genuine peer review, and its methodology is unusually sound for the genre: a stacked ensemble (ARIMA, linear regression, per-sector Random Forest, FFNN, two LSTM variants) re-estimated yearly on rolling windows — a walk-forward design, not a single fixed split — over 518 S&P 500 stocks including 67 delisted names specifically to counter survivorship bias ([arXiv 2108.10826](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.10826)). But the number is not doing what it appears to: the prediction horizon is **weekly, not daily**, and the paper's own disclosed class balance shows "up" weeks occurring in 58.19% of all index-weeks and 60.65% of the test-period index-weeks — meaning a naive always-predict-up baseline alone would already score in the high 50s/low 60s before any model skill is applied, and the authors themselves anchor their improvement claims to that inflated baseline rather than 50/50 ([arXiv 2108.10826](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.10826)). At the individual-stock level the ensemble's edge over its own best single base model is under one percentage point (62.09% vs. 61.69% for the standalone two-layer LSTM), meaning most of the headline number is attributable to the weekly horizon and skewed base rate baked into the setup, not to the ensembling mechanism — a genuine but modest result, not a clean break above the ceiling. No Sharpe ratio, precision/recall, or transaction-cost analysis is reported anywhere, and no independent replication or critique of this specific paper exists four years on.

The University of Ottawa group's two papers are a more interesting case because they share one dataset and one split, yet report 65% (published) and 72% (preprint) — and the gap is fully attributable to a disclosed architectural add-on, not a different test. All three authors — Mohammad Al Ridhawi (first/corresponding, email a University of Ottawa student address), Mahtab Haj Ali, and Hussein Al Osman (an Associate Professor whose entire 71-paper, h-index-21 career is in affective computing and multimedia, not finance) — appear only on these two linked papers; no independent publication history in financial ML exists for any of them, and the preprint's own acknowledgments thank Al Osman only for "mentorship," the phrasing of a student project, not a lab's flagship output ([Crossref DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3691980](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1109%2FACCESS.2026.3691980); [uOttawa faculty directory](https://www.uottawa.ca/faculty-engineering/school-electrical-engineering-computer-science/directory/hussein-al-osman)). The published IEEE Access paper (a node-transformer over 20 S&P 500 stocks fused with BERT sentiment via volatility-gated attention) reports **65% one-day directional accuracy [95% CI 63.2-66.8]** against a 50% random walk, 58% LSTM, and 62% plain transformer, on a single fixed 1982-2010/2011-2016/2017-2025 chronological split across the same 20 stocks used for both development and testing — and the authors' own Limitations section calls survivorship bias "the most consequential concern" and explicitly frames the whole paper as "preliminary evidence... a proof of concept," an unusually candid admission for the genre ([arXiv 2603.05917](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.05917)). IEEE Access itself is a gold-open-access "megajournal" with a single binary review round, 4-6 week turnaround, and third-party-cited acceptance-rate estimates ranging from 20% to 50% — a moderate-rigor venue, not a selective finance journal ([ieeeaccess.ieee.org](https://ieeeaccess.ieee.org/about/)). The sibling preprint adds an autoencoder-based regime router (splitting days into "normal" and "event" pathways by reconstruction error) plus a Soft Actor-Critic controller that tunes the routing threshold and blend weight, lifting the identical dataset/split/architecture's accuracy from 65% to **72%** — its own ablation table shows removing the autoencoder/regime-routing reverts the model to exactly the published paper's 0.80% MAPE, confirming the published model is literally this preprint's baseline row ([arXiv 2603.19136](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19136)). Crucially, the preprint's own Limitations section states outright that "regime learning is partly circular, though staged pre-training mitigates this" and that "frozen SAC routing is still state-dependent, so gains are partly architectural, not purely from accuracy" — the authors themselves flag that some of the 7-point jump may reflect the model's added routing flexibility fitting this one fixed test window rather than genuinely better forecasting ([arXiv 2603.19136](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19136)). As of November 2026 the preprint remains unaccepted at Applied Intelligence (Springer) with no v3, no journal reference, and zero independent citations for either paper — this is self-admitted partial circularity on an un-replicated, unreviewed result, not a confirmed exception to the ceiling.

## Lopez-Lira and Tang built the field's most rigorous edge, then proved it eats itself

Alejandro Lopez-Lira (Wharton PhD, now the Scott J. Friedman Professor of AI in Finance at the University of Florida's Warrington College) and Yuehua Tang (Georgia State PhD, *Journal of Finance*-published, incoming Warrington department chair) are the most credentialed authors in this entire batch, and their GPT-4 paper is the only one here built on a genuine formal economic model rather than a pure backtest ([UF Warrington directory](https://warrington.ufl.edu/directory/yuehua-tang); [Wharton faculty profile](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/profile/joselop)). Across 159,137 headline-firm-date observations (October 2021-May 2024) scraped and matched to RavenPack, GPT-4-0314 scored headlines YES/NO/UNKNOWN, and the paper cleanly separates two measurement windows: an "initial reaction" window (close-to-open or close-to-+15min) that the authors explicitly frame as **not tradable** because it requires trading before normal investors can react, and a "forward drift" window (open-to-close) that is the actual, implementable strategy ([arXiv 2304.07619 v6](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07619v6)). The now-famous 93.3%/88.8% hit rates belong entirely to the non-tradable reaction window; the real, tradable forward-drift hit rate is **58% overnight and 55% intraday** — exactly in line with, not above, the established ceiling. What makes this paper genuinely rigorous rather than merely well-marketed is a formal limited-attention/limits-to-arbitrage model with falsifiable, matched cross-sectional predictions: a sophistication threshold below which weak LLMs (GPT-1, GPT-2, BERT, all under 65% hit rate) generate no edge; a prediction that the edge concentrates in small-caps and after negative news (confirmed — the short leg carries Sharpe 2.01 vs. 0.78 for the long leg); and a formal mechanism (Proposition 2) by which the edge should erode as more traders adopt the same signal ([arXiv 2304.07619 v6](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07619v6)). That erosion is the paper's real headline finding and it is severe: the overnight drift strategy's annualized Sharpe ratio fell from **6.54 in 2021 Q4, to 3.68 in 2022, to 2.33 in 2023, to just 1.22 in January-May 2024** — and the strategy becomes unprofitable entirely at a 20-basis-point round-trip transaction cost, with partial-rebalancing (25% of the portfolio daily rather than 100%) the only disclosed mitigation, lifting Sharpe to 1.34 while cutting turnover from ~190%/day to ~46%/day ([arXiv 2304.07619 v6](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07619v6)). The authors hedge their own causal story ("other factors... could also contribute") but their formal model's core prediction — self-erasing edge as adoption spreads — is exactly what the data shows. Even more tellingly, the same authors' own 2025 follow-up, "The Memorization Problem," demonstrates that GPT-4o can reproduce pre-cutoff S&P 500 levels and unemployment rates to within a tenth of a percentage point from memory, and that neither "respect historical boundaries" prompting nor entity-masking prevents this — directly undercutting the sufficiency of their own flagship paper's primary leakage safeguard (simply starting the sample after the training cutoff) ([arXiv 2504.14765](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14765v2)). CUHK's newer Lookahead Propensity paper (below) independently finds a memorization channel accounting for roughly 37% of a standalone LLM predictive effect in a comparable news-headline-to-return setup, reinforcing rather than contradicting the authors' self-critique ([arXiv 2512.23847](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23847v1)). The honest verdict: this is the single best-evidenced result in the entire deep dive, but its own authors' formal model and follow-up research both say its edge is real, tradable, inside the 55-58% band — and is actively decaying by design, not a stable exception to anything.

## The 82% SPY figure is a moving-average artifact wearing a regime-switching label

Cao, Chen, Lian, and Zhang's *PLOS Complex Systems* paper is the clearest case of a number that looks like a ceiling-breaker and dissolves completely on a full-text read. The four authors (Columbia, UMass Amherst ×2, Carnegie Mellon) have no findable prior track record in quantitative finance, and the paper's own Semantic Scholar record shows zero citations and the PLOS platform shows zero reader comments fifteen months after submission ([full-text PDF](https://journals.plos.org/complexsystems/article/file?id=10.1371/journal.pcsy.0000037&type=printable); [Semantic Scholar API](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1371/journal.pcsy.0000037)). PLOS Complex Systems is a legitimate but brand-new (2023/2024-launched) nonprofit open-access journal with no Journal Impact Factor yet and a standard, non-finance-specialized editorial-board review process — this particular article did receive a named academic editor and a 13-month review-to-acceptance window, but that process did not catch several basic internal inconsistencies that survived into the published version: the paper's own text claims a "90% train / 5% test" split while its own stated observation counts (2,878 and 140 out of 3,018) actually work out to **95.4%/4.6%**; the body text reports the baseline LSTM's accuracy as 78% while Table 4 reports 79% for the same model; and the Supporting Information section references a non-existent "Sect 4.2" ([full-text PDF, Sect 3.2.1 and Table 4](https://journals.plos.org/complexsystems/article/file?id=10.1371/journal.pcsy.0000037&type=printable)). Far more consequential than these sloppiness markers is the target variable itself: SPY closing prices were smoothed with a **5-day moving average before being used as the prediction target**, and overlapping 5-day moving averages are mechanically highly autocorrelated from one day to the next — predicting the direction of consecutive smoothed values is a structurally easier task than predicting raw daily returns, likely the single largest driver of the 82% figure, and the paper neither acknowledges nor controls for this ([full-text PDF, Sect 3.2.1](https://journals.plos.org/complexsystems/article/file?id=10.1371/journal.pcsy.0000037&type=printable)). The evaluation itself is a single fixed, non-walk-forward split, tested on exactly one 140-trading-day out-of-sample window (roughly mid-to-late 2023) — a period of a trending, low-volatility SPY rally that would flatter almost any model with even mild trend-following bias — and there is no evidence the paper controls for whether its min-max scaling bounds were computed on the full series (spanning into the test period) or training data only. The "volatility-regime-switching DLSTM" framing in the paper's own title is also considerably weaker than it sounds: the Copper-Gold-Ratio "regime" component is never given any predictive-accuracy metric at all — it is purely a narrative correlation analysis of four hindsight-selected historical crisis windows — and there is no programmatic rule anywhere in the paper that actually switches between the CGR and DLSTM approaches; "regime-switching" describes two separate, never-integrated analyses stitched together by a conceptual narrative, not a tested switching architecture. No Sharpe ratio or buy-and-hold benchmark accompanies the paper's $500-to-$562-over-60-days trading simulation, making even that number uninterpretable against a contemporaneous SPY rally. This is, point for point, the clearest non-survivor among all eight items in this deep dive.

## TradingAgents' debate architecture loses to buy-and-hold once costs are applied honestly

TradingAgents began as Yijia Xiao's UCLA PhD project (advised by Wei Wang, Tsinghua BS) and has since grown into Tauric Research, a small commercial "market intelligence" company selling an API/MCP platform — but the architecture's headline backtest numbers do not survive independent reproduction ([alphaxiv researcher profile](https://www.alphaxiv.org/researchers/yijia-xiao); [arXiv 2412.20138](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20138)). The paper's layered pipeline — four specialized Analysts feeding a Bull/Bear Researcher debate, a Trader, a three-way Risk Management debate (risk-seeking/neutral/conservative), and a final Fund Manager synthesis, built on LangGraph with a quick-thinking/deep-thinking (gpt-4o-mini/gpt-4o vs. o1-preview) LLM split — reported AAPL cumulative return **26.62%** (Sharpe 8.21), GOOGL **24.36%** (Sharpe 6.39), and AMZN **23.21%** (Sharpe 5.60), each beating five rule-based baselines on every metric, but the test covered only three tickers over a single three-month window (January-March 2024), and the paper itself flags its own Sharpe ratios as anomalous, attributing the implausibly smooth returns to "few pullbacks... during that period" — i.e., the authors concede the test window was unusually favorable ([arXiv 2412.20138 v7](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.20138v7)). No win rate and no internal ablation isolating the debate mechanism itself (versus simply having more analyst agents or a stronger reasoning model) was found anywhere in the paper. Independently, "The Alpha Illusion" (Ye et al., arXiv 2605.16895, May 2026) reproduced a TradingAgents-style system over a roughly one-year 2025-2026 window with realistic costs modeled, and found the reported gains **largely evaporated**: Sharpe fell from **0.43 to 0.22 after costs**, with performance dropping **below buy-and-hold** once commissions, inference cost, bid-ask spread, and market impact were applied — the authors explicitly recommend treating reported LLM-trading-agent alpha as "historical backtest/prototype evidence," never "deployment evidence" ([alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.16895](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.16895)). Two further independent critiques (FINSABER, arXiv 2505.07078; Yao & Zheng, arXiv 2606.08285) separately argue the entire category — TradingAgents named explicitly — is systematically evaluated on narrow timeframes and small universes that overstate effectiveness via survivorship and data-snooping bias, and that unclear timing conventions and omitted transaction costs make even architecturally elegant multi-agent systems empirically fragile ([arXiv 2505.07078](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07078v1); [arXiv 2606.08285](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.08285.pdf)). Most tellingly, the maintainers' own GitHub issue #805 (opened May 2026) is a sustained, multi-month internal admission trail: after a roughly six-release campaign (v0.3.0 through v0.7.1) of fixing explicit data-level look-ahead bugs across news, FRED, SEC EDGAR, and insider-filing feeds, maintainer Yijia-Xiao directly concedes that **LLM pretraining-knowledge contamination is "a separate, fundamental limitation a code change can't fully eliminate"** — leaving the issue open rather than closed — and a community member's own empirical test (3,708 earnings announcements, 250 S&P 500 companies, 2023-vs-2024 comparison) found detectable recall/contamination in 10 of 12 tested models, with AUCs of 0.69-0.90 for the top four ([GitHub Issue #805](https://github.com/TauricResearch/TradingAgents/issues/805)). No independent source was found that replicates and confirms the original paper's outperformance under realistic conditions — every piece of independent follow-up work is critical or debunking in direction.

## The newest 2025-2026 papers split sharply between a durable diagnostic and three fragile claims

Among the four most recent papers, the strongest and most durable is CUHK Business School's Lookahead Propensity (LAP) detector (Zhenyu Gao, Wenxi Jiang, Yutong Yan), which probes whether a model "already knows" an answer by asking it, given only a ticker and date with no news attached, whether a return went up or down — LAP = P(up) + P(down) from first-token probabilities ([arXiv 2512.23847](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23847)). Testing Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct on 91,357 in-sample headline-return pairs and 7,568 genuinely post-cutoff (2024) pairs, the contamination-amplification interaction is large and significant in-sample (t = 3.64) and collapses to statistical insignificance post-cutoff (t = 1.06) — specifically because mean LAP itself collapses from 0.414 in-sample to roughly 7×10⁻⁶ post-cutoff, meaning the model simply refuses to commit to a direction once asked about a genuinely future date ([arXiv 2512.23847](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23847)). This is a clean, mechanistic, reusable diagnostic — not a performance claim — and it is the one piece of this four-paper batch not undermined by its own authors or independent critics. The other three are considerably shakier. MemGuard-Alpha (Anisha Roy and Dip Roy, IIT Patna) is a genuine self-correction case: version 1 reported that its five-method membership-inference composite score, filtered into a trading strategy, lifted Sharpe from 2.76 to **4.11** (a 49% improvement); version 2, retitled from "Detecting and Filtering..." to "**Limits of** Membership Inference...," reports the opposite — that a key "temporal-proximity" feature achieving AUC 1.000 is a tautological artifact of being a monotone transform of the label itself, that raw MIA scores collapse to chance level (0.487-0.537) once properly normalized within-model, and that under symmetric transaction costs **no filtering variant beats the unfiltered ensemble**, while simply dropping the single weakest model outperforms every contamination-based filter ([arXiv 2603.26797 via alphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.26797)). Chen and Pu's agentic nowcaster (Guanghua School of Management, Peking University) is the one genuinely live, prospective (not backtested) design in this batch — nine months of real-time daily predictions from April 2025 using an undisclosed LLM's native web search over X/Twitter and news — and its most robust finding (monotonic alpha decay from Top-10/20 through Top-100 to statistically insignificant Bottom-20, meaning the agent can identify winners but not losers) is genuinely clean ([arXiv 2601.11958](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11958)). But its own headline numbers are internally contradictory and unresolved within the same paper: the abstract/main text reports Top-20 daily alpha of **18.4 bps/day** (Sharpe 2.43), while the paper's own Table 5 reports **33.4 bps/day** (Sharpe 3.23) for what is described as the identical portfolio — a conflict the authors never reconcile ([arXiv 2601.11958](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11958)). Finally, "When Agents Disagree: The Selection Bottleneck" (Artem Maryanskyy, Uber Technologies; Dmitry Budnikov, Federal Scientific Agroengineering Center VIM; Alibek T. Kaliyev, UT Austin) is an arXiv-only, non-finance preprint — all 42 evaluation tasks are general coding/reasoning/ethics/science/summarization tasks, none finance-related — but its confirmed numbers are the most statistically airtight in this entire deep dive: judge-based selection over a diverse model team scored **0.810** [95% CI 0.768, 0.851] against a single-Opus baseline, majority voting over the same diverse team scored **0.496** [0.425, 0.563] (statistically indistinguishable from the 0.500 single-model parity line), and homogeneous-team-plus-judge scored only **0.512** — meaning diversity without a judge is nearly worthless, and the diversity-effect-vs-homogeneous Hedges' g = 2.71 (p = 7.55×10⁻¹⁵) and vote-vs-homogeneous Δ = -0.016 (p = 0.669) are both large-sample, Holm-Bonferroni-adjusted, and robust across 38 of 42 individual tasks with no reversals ([arXiv 2603.20324](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.20324)). The authors' own disclosed caveat — Claude Sonnet serving as judge while also appearing in the generation pool alongside Claude Opus, raising a same-family self-evaluation-bias risk — is the one qualifier that matters for anyone importing this result directly.

## Conclusion: build the judge and the leakage probe, skip the debate theater and the naive filter

Laid end to end, these eight efforts converge on a counterintuitive but load-bearing pattern: every number that exceeds the 55-66% ceiling does so by changing what is being measured (weekly vs. daily, smoothed vs. raw target, reaction vs. drift, in-sample vs. post-cutoff, pre-cost vs. post-cost), while every number that holds up under a stable measurement collapses back inside the band — and the one unambiguous winner in the whole batch is not an accuracy claim at all, but a mechanism result from a general-reasoning study. For a Gemini-based three-call consensus engine sitting at ~34% exact decision accuracy and ~54% overall direction accuracy (81% on SPY, ~68s latency), this sharpens rather than overturns the earlier report's recommendations in three concrete ways. First, replacing majority voting with judge-based selection moves from "worth testing" to "strongly evidenced," given the Selection Bottleneck study's p = 0.669 finding that voting adds nothing over a single model — but the same-family caveat means the judge should come from a different model vendor than whatever generates the three candidate calls, and because none of the study's 42 tasks involve delayed, probabilistic financial ground truth, the team's own pilot (judging historical-calibration fit, not "which reasoning reads better") is the right adaptation, not a blind port of the 0.810 figure. Second, a cheap LAP-style self-check — asking the same model a stripped-down "ticker + date, up/down/unknown" recall query and flagging high first-token confidence on dates that should postdate its training cutoff — is now worth building directly into the pipeline; it is the one technique in this entire deep dive with a clean, reproducible mechanism and no internal reversal. Third, and most important as a subtraction: multi-agent debate architectures (TradingAgents' Bull/Bear/risk-committee pattern) and composite memorization-filters (MemGuard-Alpha) both looked like obvious next builds from the earlier shallow pass and are now revealed as weaker than they appeared — the former loses to buy-and-hold after costs in independent reproduction and has no internal ablation ever showing debate itself (versus just more LLM calls) drives any gain, and the latter was retracted by its own authors between v1 and v2. Naive daily sentiment aggregation, Sharpe ratios computed on single short trending windows, and any accuracy claim that doesn't disclose its class balance or test-window length should all be treated with the same skepticism the deep dive applies to the PLOS and Al Ridhawi papers — the discipline this report rewards, again and again, is not a cleverer architecture but a harder, more honest look at what the test actually measured.
