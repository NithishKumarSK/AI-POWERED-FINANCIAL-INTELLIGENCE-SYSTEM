# Stock-direction prediction rarely clears sixty percent

The most important finding across academic research, commercial products, and the world's most secretive quant funds is the same: nobody credible and independently verified has demonstrated reliable stock-direction prediction accuracy much above **55–66%** on a genuinely out-of-sample, 1-day-ahead basis, and the handful of claims above 80–90% either measure something other than forward prediction, come from unaudited vendor marketing, or collapse once tested on data the model couldn't have memorized. Against that backdrop, this project's own measured results — **~35% exact 3-way match** (BUY/SELL/HOLD) on 475 real walk-forward predictions, **~55% directional (sign-only) accuracy**, **~10.5 percentage-point average return error**, and a separately-evaluated **~85.6% price-range containment rate** across 125 cases — are not an outlier below the field; they sit almost exactly at the median of what rigorous, leakage-controlled academic studies report for individual stocks and indices, and they compare favorably to nearly every commercial "AI stock-picking" product whose claims could be checked against independent data. The one caveat that applies to every number in this report, including the project's own, is scale: 475 and 125 are samples large enough to be taken seriously but still far smaller than the "few hundred to low-thousands" threshold the quant-industry literature treats as the point where an edge becomes statistically distinguishable from a lucky run. No major quant fund (Renaissance, Two Sigma, Citadel, D.E. Shaw) has ever disclosed a verified per-prediction hit rate at all, and Numerai — the most transparent quant-crowdsourcing platform in existence — deliberately avoids "accuracy" as a metric because real, monetizable market edges are small rank-correlations, not clean percentages. The report below lays out exactly which figures are independently verified, which are self-reported marketing, and which have no real figure at all, then ranks the project against that honest baseline.

## The academic ceiling sits at 50–66%, not the headlines

Peer-reviewed and arXiv-hosted studies that use proper walk-forward or chronologically-split out-of-sample evaluation converge tightly on a **50–66% directional-accuracy band** for 1-day-ahead up/down prediction on individual stocks and major indices — essentially a coin flip with a small, sometimes real edge. Zhong & Hitchcock's Random Forest + LSTM ensemble, combining technical, fundamental, and text data, reported **66.18% accuracy at the S&P 500 index level and 62.09% for individual stocks** ([arXiv 2108.10826](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.10826)), and a 2026 transformer-plus-BERT-sentiment model reached **65% one-day accuracy with disclosed 95% bootstrap confidence intervals**, against a 58% plain-LSTM baseline, 62% plain-transformer baseline, and 50% random baseline ([arXiv 2603.05917](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.05917)). These two results mark the top of the credible range found anywhere in the literature. Below that ceiling, the bulk of rigorous studies land much closer to chance: a walk-forward comparison of LSTM vs. ARIMA on Tesla found LSTM's directional edge was **50.60% vs. ARIMA's 47.62%** ([MEST Journal](https://www.meste.org/ojs/index.php/mest/article/view/1575)); a 2015–2023 study found **51.22%** for its primary model versus **48.17%** for a Transformer baseline ([DSpace Ouargla](https://dspace.univ-ouargla.dz/jspui/handle/123456789/40981?mode=full)); and a 2026 study across CSI 300, ChiNext, and CSI 500 found the best model hit **51.74% accuracy with an AUC of just 0.52** — barely distinguishable from chance ([Atlantis Press](https://www.atlantis-press.com/proceedings/icssed-26/126026271)). A feature-selection survey tabulating results across many papers found reported directional accuracies ranging from roughly 44% to 100%, with one specific well-cited study (Xie et al., 2021) at **53.3%–57.4%** ([Springer jfin-swufe](https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40854-022-00441-7/tables/2)).

Crucially, several of these same studies warn that even figures in the 55–60% range can be illusory: one 2026 arXiv paper found its "smart" model scored **57.0% accuracy at a 30-day horizon against a naive base-rate of 59.9%** — meaning the sophisticated model actually underperformed simply always picking the majority class. Zoltan Lopez-Lira and Yuehua Tang's rigorous GPT-4 study, which deliberately tested only on data published after the model's training cutoff to rule out look-ahead bias, illustrates the gap between a flashy headline number and a real, tradable one with unusual clarity: GPT-4's hit rate for predicting the market's *instantaneous reaction* to already-published news hit **93.3% (overnight) and 88.8% (intraday)** — the number the paper's abstract highlights — but the *actual forward-looking, tradable* price-drift hit rate over the next 1–2 trading days was only **58% (overnight) and 55% (intraday)**, with Sharpe ratios that declined from 6.54 in late 2021 to 1.22 by early 2024, and the authors state outright that the strategy becomes **unprofitable at a 20-basis-point round-trip transaction cost** ([arXiv 2304.07619](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07619v6)). That 55–58% tradable, forward-looking figure — not the 90%+ headline number — is the fair comparison point for any system claiming genuine forecasting skill, and it sits just below this project's own 55% directional match. At the other extreme, a Strathmore University thesis claiming **95%/96% accuracy** for S&P 500/NASDAQ LSTM models is flagged here explicitly as methodologically unverified: it is an unpublished thesis with no disclosed leakage controls, and it is a dramatic outlier against every peer-reviewed or arXiv result found ([Strathmore repository](https://su-plus.strathmore.edu/items/c5835860-8dea-40e0-ace1-61d5ec471571)). No architecture — LSTM, transformer, Random Forest, ARIMA, or LLM — consistently dominates across studies, and several papers (Coolsaet et al.'s TSX transformer work, Kesri & Salhi's Tesla study) explicitly report cases where a model with a better statistical loss or accuracy score did *not* win on actual simulated trading returns ([arXiv 2605.23962](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/arxpapers/2605.23962.htm)) — a pattern directly relevant to interpreting any accuracy-vs-return-error framing. No academic paper found reports a directly comparable "exact 3-way buy/sell/hold match" metric or a percentage-point return-error metric matching this project's framing, which is a genuine structural gap in the literature rather than a search failure.

## LLM-based forecasters face their own, newly-documented leakage trap

Because this project's prediction engine is itself LLM-based (Gemini), the single most load-bearing academic finding for honest self-assessment is "Profit Mirage: Revisiting Information Leakage in LLM-based Financial Agents" (arXiv:2510.07920, October 2025). The paper tested GPT-4o-based trading agents on a period before the model's training cutoff and again on a period after it, with similar overall market conditions in both windows (roughly +13.5–13.8% market return each) specifically to rule out "it was just a harder regime" as an excuse. Backtested performance **decayed by 50–72%** once the agents were forced to forecast genuinely unseen, post-cutoff data — total-return decay ranged from **50.18% (TradingAgents) to 71.85% (FinMem)**, and Sharpe-ratio decay ranged from **51.48% (QuantAgent) to 62.23% (FinCON)** — and the authors describe the best-performing agent's apparent edge as becoming "a statistical zero." A companion memorization audit found the underlying LLMs could answer historical-market-fact questions with **85–93% accuracy purely from pretraining memorization**, not genuine forecasting, and a fine-tuning experiment showed in-distribution accuracy jumping from 51.61% to 72.16% (Qwen2.5-7B) and 54.73% to 76.52% (Llama-3.1-8B) while true out-of-distribution forecasting accuracy *fell* by 18–21.5 percentage points over the same change ([arXiv 2510.07920](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07920v1)). This is the closest thing in the literature to a controlled experiment proving that an LLM-based predictor's apparent skill can be entirely memorization rather than forecasting — and it sets the standard any LLM-driven system, including this one, should be measured against: are the 475 test predictions drawn from a period the underlying model could have memorized, or genuinely out-of-sample relative to its knowledge cutoff? The broader backtest-overfitting literature backs up why this matters at scale — Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu's "Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial Charlatanism" demonstrated a strategy with an in-sample Sharpe ratio of 1.59 whose true out-of-sample Sharpe ratio was **-0.18** ([mathinvestor.org](https://mathinvestor.org/?p=1571)), and a Quantopian study of 888 real user-submitted trading algorithms found in-sample Sharpe explained essentially none of out-of-sample performance (R² < 0.025), with the gap between in-sample and out-of-sample performance *growing* the more a user iterated on backtesting the same strategy ([Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/quantopians-academic-paper-about-in-vs-out-of-sample-performance-of-trading-alg/)). A 2019 editorial opening a themed *Algorithmic Finance* issue on information leakage states that **more than half of submissions** to financial-ML venues contain leakage errors — commonly survivorship bias, look-ahead/target leakage, or scaling data using the full sample before splitting ([SAGE Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.3233/AF-190900)) — making any unverified 70%+ accuracy claim a candidate for exactly this failure mode until proven otherwise.

## Commercial AI trading products sell certainty they cannot independently prove

Across every retail "AI stock prediction" product examined — EquBot's AIEQ, Kavout's K Score, Trade Ideas' Holly AI, Tickeron, Danelfin, I Know First, and Vantagepoint A.I. — the pattern is structurally identical: every specific accuracy or win-rate percentage traces back to the vendor's own website, press release, or an affiliate review site with a financial incentive to relay it favorably, and no outside academic, regulatory, or journalistic body was found to have independently replicated or audited any of these figures. Tickeron publishes an **85–92% accuracy** claim for its short-interval trading agents, but an independent reviewer states plainly that these "self-reported accuracy stats are not independently audited," and other independent reviews describe real-world pattern-recognition results as "mixed" or "inconsistent" ([Liberated Stock Trader](https://www.liberatedstocktrader.com/tickeron-review/); [WalletInvestor](https://walletinvestor.com/news/finance-news/tickeron-review-are-pre-built-ai-trading-bots-reliable-for-pattern-recognition/)). I Know First's widely-circulated **"97% accuracy"** figure is a company claim relayed by Benzinga "according to the company," with no independent audit found and promotional pages favoring cherry-picked individual results like "81% in seven days" over a full track record ([Benzinga](https://benzinga.com/z/14482361)). Trade Ideas advertises Holly AI win rates in the "low-to-mid sixties percent" and posts same-day in-progress tallies like "5 wins out of 7 trades" with no disclosure of whether the trades were live, simulated, or backtested; an independent review site reports that "users report that live, real-money results come in lower" ([Liberated Stock Trader](https://www.liberatedstocktrader.com/trade-ideas-review/)). Kavout's own published backtest is internally the most revealing: its US "Top Picks" portfolio (K Score 7–9, 2012–2024) posted a **12.94% CAGR versus 14.93% for the S&P 500** — a **-1.54% alpha**, i.e., underperformance — directly contradicting the company's separate marketing claim that the product "has out-performed the S&P 500" ([Kavout](https://kavout.com/product/kscore-us-top500-stocks-by-market-cap)).

The one partial exception is EquBot's AIEQ, an SEC-registered, publicly-traded ETF whose actual investment returns — unlike every other product's internal "accuracy" metric — are tracked by independent data providers (Morningstar, Bloomberg, Yahoo Finance) regardless of what the company claims. That independently verifiable record is unflattering to the company's own narrative: Yahoo Finance (citing Morningstar) showed AIEQ's **3-year annualized return at 4.8% versus 9.1% for VTI**, and its **5-year annualized return at 5.5% versus 8.6%** ([Yahoo Finance](https://money.yahoo.com/move-over-chatgpt-ai-powered-221500544.html)); a January 2022 retrospective found AIEQ up **57% since its October 2017 inception versus 84% for the S&P 500 Total Return Index** over the same span ([Tech Brew](https://www.techbrew.com/stories/2022/01/26/how-the-world-s-first-ai-managed-etf-stacks-up-almost-five-years-later)); and Motley Fool directly contradicted EquBot's own CIO claim of having "beat our benchmark" in year two by reporting that in 2018 the S&P 500 lost about 6% while AIEQ lost nearly 16% ([Motley Fool via Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/ai-powered-etf-failed-miserably-beating-market-2018-heres-what-you-can-learn-its-mistakes)). CXO Advisory, an evidence-based investing research site, ran its own long-horizon comparison of AIEQ against SPY from inception and concluded broadly that "very limited available evidence offers little support for belief that an AI-based equity fund can beat the market" ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=30628)) — the closest thing to a genuinely independent, non-vendor-funded empirical verdict found anywhere in this research, and it lands against the AI product, not for it.

## The world's best quant funds hide hit rates behind aggregate returns

No major quantitative hedge fund — Renaissance Technologies, Two Sigma, Citadel, or D.E. Shaw — has ever officially, firm-sourced disclosed a specific per-prediction stock-direction accuracy or hit-rate percentage; for three of those four (Two Sigma, Citadel, D.E. Shaw), **no figure of any kind was found**, and that absence itself is informative given how widely the one circulating number for Renaissance has been repeated. That number — Medallion fund being "right 50.75% of the time" — is a secondhand anecdote attributed to former co-CEO Robert Mercer in Gregory Zuckerman's book *The Man Who Solved the Market*, not an audited or firm-published statistic, and Mercer's own framing of it ("we're 100 percent right 50.75 percent of the time") emphasizes that the profit comes from consistency and volume, not high per-trade accuracy ([Goodreads notes on *The Man Who Solved the Market*](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/44698571-the-man-who-solved-the-market/58027759-thomas-wolf)). What *is* independently documented is Medallion's aggregate return: UCLA finance professor Bradford Cornell's analysis found an average gross annual return of **66.1% (39.2% net)** from 1988–2018, with a standard deviation of 31.7% gross — a figure so large that Cornell concludes it "stretches to the limit" ordinary explanations of skill, while Renaissance itself declined to comment on the methodology behind it ([Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bswymr8cih3jeaslxc00/portfolio/famed-medallion-fund-stretches-explanation-to-the-limit-professor-claims)). This non-disclosure is not idiosyncratic secrecy; an academic study of investor letters from 434 hedge funds found that **better-performing funds systematically disclose less quantitative detail**, consistent with a "proprietary cost" theory in which revealing methodology lets competitors free-ride on the fund's research ([HU Berlin seminar paper](https://www.wiwi.hu-berlin.de/en/Professorships/bwl/finance/seminars/papers-fin-acc-res-seminars/16s/cassar.pdf)).

Numerai — the most transparent quant-crowdsourcing platform that exists, with every scoring formula published — reinforces the same lesson from the opposite direction: it deliberately does not use a binary "accuracy" metric at all. Its headline metric, CORR ("Numerai Corr"), is a rank correlation between a model's predictions and forward stock returns, not a hit rate ([Numerai docs](https://docs.numer.ai/numerai-tournament/scoring/correlation-corr)), and when a community member directly asked what counts as a "good" score, a Numerai forum response stated flatly: "there is no real good answer to that question" ([Numerai forum](https://forum.numer.ai/t/what-is-a-good-sharpe-ratio-and-validation-correlation/419)). Informal community consensus — not official Numerai documentation — places a real signal around **CORR 0.02–0.03**, against a theoretical live ceiling estimated around **0.468** ([Numerai forum](https://forum.numer.ai/t/submission-core-metrics/744)). The fact that the single most transparent, best-documented quant-signal marketplace in the industry still can't agree on what a "good score" looks like, and refuses to translate its metric into a percentage, is itself strong evidence that real, monetizable market edges are structurally small and don't map cleanly onto the "accuracy percentage" framing that commercial products use to market themselves.

## Why a raw accuracy percentage is close to meaningless without context

Three pieces of context from the quant-industry literature determine whether any accuracy figure in this report — including the project's own — means anything at all. First, baselines: a balanced binary up/down classifier has an expected chance accuracy of **50%**, and a balanced 3-way buy/sell/hold classifier has an expected chance accuracy of **33.3%** ([cvms documentation](https://rdrr.io/cran/cvms/man/baseline_multinomial.html)), but real market data is often class-imbalanced (more "hold" or "up" days than the alternative), so the *true* no-skill bar a system must clear can be higher than the naive chance number — meaning any cross-system comparison that doesn't disclose class balance in its test set is not fully apples-to-apples. Second, payoff asymmetry: profitability is governed by **expectancy** — (win rate × average win) minus (loss rate × average loss) — not win rate in isolation, and the breakeven win rate is **1/(1 + reward-to-risk ratio)**: about 33% at a 2:1 payoff, 25% at 3:1 ([LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/blog/risk-reward-ratio-vs-win-rate-key-differences-2/)). A real backtest of a Turtle-style breakout system on EUR/USD had a **40% win rate and still lost money**, falling just short of its own 41% breakeven threshold despite a seemingly respectable hit rate ([Backtrex](https://backtrex.com/en/backtests/turtle-trading-eur-usd)) — concrete proof that a bare win-rate or accuracy number, without the payoff ratio attached, cannot be judged as "good" or "bad" in isolation. Third, sample size: a reported accuracy figure is only as trustworthy as its confidence interval, which shrinks with the square root of sample size. Applying the standard binomial approximation: at **n=30**, a reported 55% accuracy has a roughly **37%–73%** 95% confidence interval — statistically indistinguishable from a coin flip; at **n=100**, that interval narrows to roughly **45%–65%**, still overlapping 50%; only at **n=1,000** does a 55% figure narrow to roughly **52%–58%**, clearly distinguishable from chance. Industry practitioner guidance converges on wanting **"a few hundred trades spanning more than one market regime"** before treating a win rate as a stable property of a strategy rather than an artifact of the sample ([LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/win-rate/)); a concrete illustrative benchmark states that a 65% win rate over 20 trades is explicitly *not* statistically significant, while the same 65% over 200 trades *is* ([Aeromir](https://futures.aeromir.com/montecarlohelp)). This project's 475 walk-forward predictions clear that "low hundreds" threshold comfortably for the directional and 3-way metrics; its 125-case range-containment test sits right at the edge of it — large enough to be a genuine signal, but not yet at the scale (low thousands) where the quant literature would call the number fully settled.

## Where the project honestly ranks

Measured against this landscape, the project's **~55% directional accuracy** is not a weak result — it lands almost exactly where Lopez-Lira & Tang's rigorously-tested, forward-looking GPT-4 tradable signal landed (55–58%), comfortably above the near-chance 50–52% cluster found in several walk-forward academic studies (Tesla's 50.60%, CSI 300's 51.74%, the Ouargla study's 51.22%), and not far below the best documented academic results anywhere in this research (Zhong & Hitchcock's 62–66%, Al Ridhawi's 65%). Given that several papers explicitly warn that even headline figures in this range can reflect base-rate/class-imbalance artifacts rather than genuine skill, the honest read is that 55% places the project inside the legitimate literature's main cluster rather than at either extreme — a real, modest, plausible edge, not an inflated or suspicious one. The **~35% exact 3-way match** should be read against a 33.3% chance baseline for a balanced buy/sell/hold split; at that bar it represents only a small margin over chance, and no academic paper found in this research reports a directly comparable 3-way categorical benchmark, so there is no apples-to-apples academic figure to rank it against — this is a genuine gap in the comparison, not a case where the project is being judged against a hidden better number. The **10.5 percentage-point average return error** likewise has no directly comparable published figure, since most academic papers report RMSE/MAE on different scales rather than an "average error in predicted return vs. actual return" in percentage-point terms.

Against the commercial product category, the comparison is more favorable still: this project's numbers come from internally-measured, disclosed walk-forward testing on a known sample size (475 cases), whereas EquBot, Kavout, Trade Ideas, Tickeron, Danelfin, I Know First, and Vantagepoint A.I. all either publish no real accuracy figure, publish a figure that is unaudited and vendor-sourced, or — in Kavout's case — publish a backtest that contradicts their own marketing. Where an independently verifiable commercial benchmark does exist (AIEQ's actual ETF returns), that benchmark has underperformed the S&P 500 across most multi-year windows examined, which is a materially weaker, independently-confirmed outcome than a system posting a disclosed 55% directional hit rate. Against the quant-fund tier, direct comparison is structurally impossible: none of the four elite funds researched have ever disclosed a verified hit-rate number at all, Renaissance's one circulating figure is unconfirmed anecdote, and Numerai's entire design philosophy treats "accuracy percentage" as the wrong unit of measurement for real market edges — these organizations make their money from expectancy and volume on tiny statistical edges (50.75% win rate, single-digit percent rank correlation) applied at enormous scale and with professional-grade risk control, not from a headline accuracy number a retail system could ever match or be meaningfully ranked against. The project's separate **85.6% price-range containment** result across 125 cases is the hardest of its four metrics to benchmark, since no academic or commercial source in this research reports a comparable min/max range-forecasting accuracy metric; it should be read as a genuinely different task (interval containment, not point/direction prediction) with its own, looser chance baseline, and its 125-case sample sits at the edge of, rather than comfortably past, the "low hundreds" threshold the quant literature treats as minimally trustworthy.

## Conclusion

The clearest lesson from this research is that the entire category of "stock prediction accuracy" is far more honest and far more modest than either academic abstracts or commercial marketing pages suggest once methodology is checked: the real, survivable ceiling for genuinely forward-looking, leakage-free 1-day-ahead prediction is roughly 55–66%, elite quant funds win not through high per-trade accuracy but through tiny, consistent edges applied at massive scale with strict risk management, and the retail "AI stock-picking" industry has, with the partial exception of one underperforming ETF, produced no independently verifiable accuracy figure at all. Seen against that backdrop, this project's 55% directional accuracy and 35% exact 3-way match are neither inflated nor embarrassing — they are honestly-measured, real walk-forward numbers that sit inside the same band the most careful academic studies occupy, a band that the commercial AI-trading industry routinely claims to exceed without ever producing comparable, checkable evidence. The clearest next step for strengthening the project's own claims, following directly from the "Profit Mirage" and backtest-overfitting literature, is to explicitly confirm and document that the 475 test predictions were evaluated on data the underlying Gemini model could not have memorized from pretraining, and to keep growing the walk-forward test set toward the "few hundred to low-thousands" sample size the quant-industry literature treats as the point where an edge stops being statistically ambiguous — the project is already past the threshold where a 55% figure stops looking like noise, but it is not yet at the scale where that conclusion is beyond dispute.
