# Academic Benchmark Studies on Short-to-Medium-Horizon Stock Direction Prediction

## What direction-prediction accuracy do credible, properly-evaluated academic papers report for individual stocks or major indices?

### Takeaway
Across rigorous, out-of-sample studies on individual large-cap stocks (Tesla), U.S. indices (S&P 500, Dow Jones), and Chinese indices (CSI 300/500, ChiNext), reported directional ("up/down") accuracy clusters tightly in the **50–66% range** on 1-day-ahead horizons, with the most careful daily-return studies landing closest to 50–55%. This is the band the real-world project's ~55% directional match should be benchmarked against — it sits squarely within, or even above, what rigorous academic work on individual names typically reports.

### Cited Findings
- Fischer & Krauss (2018), *European Journal of Operational Research* 270(2), 654–669, "Deep learning with long short-term memory networks for financial market predictions": used LSTM to predict out-of-sample directional moves of S&P 500 constituent stocks from 1992–2015 using survivorship-bias-free constituent lists. I could not extract the exact headline hit-rate percentage from the PDF (binary/compressed, not machine-readable via fetch), but the paper's claimed average annualized return was >200% before transaction costs and 82% after — [working paper PDF](https://iwf.rw.fau.de/files/2015/12/11-2017.pdf); DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2017.11.054. **Flagged as a gap**: exact accuracy % not independently confirmed in this research pass.
- A follow-up paper explicitly calls Fischer & Krauss's >200%/82% annualized returns "not maintainable in the long run," and finds that after 2008 all three compared models' average daily returns fall consistently below 0.1%, "which confirms the result of Fischer and Krauss (2018) which find a deteriorating performance of LSTM models" — cited via [arXiv search result, paper unspecified exact ID].
- An independent replication-style study of the S&P 500 (1997–2018) found the LSTM's classification accuracy was "worse than previously reported" versus the original Fischer & Krauss claims, though it "still contained statistically significant predicting power" — [source located via search, specific arXiv/thesis ID not confirmed in this pass; treat as secondary].
- Zhong & Hitchcock, "S&P 500 Stock Price Prediction Using Technical, Fundamental and Text Data" — reported **66.18% accuracy** for S&P 500 index-level directional prediction and **62.09% accuracy** for individual-stock directional prediction, combining Random Forest and LSTM — [arXiv 2108.10826](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.10826) (also mirrored at [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.10826)).
- A University of Ouargla study (2015–2023 data) reported a **51.22% directional accuracy** for its primary model vs. **48.17% for a Transformer** baseline — both close to chance — [DSpace record](https://dspace.univ-ouargla.dz/jspui/handle/123456789/40981?mode=full).
- A 2026 EAI conference paper using 2013–2024 S&P 500 data found LSTM had higher accuracy than logistic regression, but noted the AUC values of both models were "close to stochastic levels" (i.e., near chance) — [EAI DOI 10.4108/eai.22-5-2026.2365120](https://eudl.eu/doi/10.4108/eai.22-5-2026.2365120).
- A Strathmore University (Kenya) thesis reported LSTM accuracy of **95% (S&P 500) and 96% (NASDAQ)** — flagged explicitly as suspect: this is a thesis (not peer-reviewed), the figure is far outside the range of every other paper found, and no robust out-of-sample/leakage controls were confirmed — [Strathmore repository](https://su-plus.strathmore.edu/items/c5835860-8dea-40e0-ace1-61d5ec471571).
- Tesla-specific: Xiao, Deng & Bi (2024) report **94% "accuracy"** for an LSTM on Tesla data (2015–2024), but the metric definition is unclear/undefined in the paper and reads like it may not be a clean directional out-of-sample hit rate — [arXiv 2411.05790](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.05790). Flagged as low-confidence due to ambiguous metric definition.
- Tesla-specific, more rigorous: Kesri & Salhi (MEST Journal) used walk-forward validation comparing LSTM to ARIMA on Tesla stock. ARIMA had better point-forecast accuracy (RMSE $8.30, MAE $5.76); LSTM's directional accuracy edge was **50.60% vs. ARIMA's 47.62%** — both close to a coin flip — [MEST Journal, Kesri & Salhi](https://www.meste.org/ojs/index.php/mest/article/view/1575).
- Chinese indices: a 2026 study of CSI 300, ChiNext, and CSI 500 daily data (2015–2025) found LSTM was the best of compared models with **directional accuracy of 51.74%, AUC 0.52** — barely above chance — [Atlantis Press ICSSED 2026](https://www.atlantis-press.com/proceedings/icssed-26/126026271).
- A feature-selection survey table lists a range of reported "direction prediction" accuracies from roughly **44% to 100%** across studies, with Xie et al. (2021) specifically cited at **53.3%–57.4%** — [Springer jfin-swufe table](https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40854-022-00441-7/tables/2).
- Becker & Leschinski (2018), Leibniz University Hannover working paper, "Directional Predictability of Daily Stock Returns": argues Dow Jones daily-return direction has been predictable over a 15-year window using conventional short-term factors with proper out-of-sample forecasting method selection, and notes "simple forecasting methods have outperformed more complex machine learning" — summarized via [Macrosynergy](https://macrosynergy.com/?p=40742); original at [IDEAS/RePEc dp-624](https://ideas.repec.org/p/han/dpaper/dp-624.html). Note: a specific "51.99% logistic regression hit rate" figure I searched for could not be confirmed as belonging to this paper in this research pass — treat that specific number as unconfirmed.
- A 2026 arXiv paper (name/ID not confirmed) explicitly warns that high directional accuracy can just reflect the base rate of up-days vs. down-days rather than real skill: at a 30-day horizon its model scored 57.0% accuracy against a **naive base-rate of 59.9%** (i.e., the "smart" model was worse than just always predicting the majority class) — found via search snippet, original paper ID unconfirmed.
- A forex/stock deep-learning survey found reported accuracies spanning "40–50% up to 90–100%," noting most CNN/LSTM papers fall between 50–80%, with one standout paper (unnamed in the snippet) claiming 90–100% — [arXiv 2103.09750](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.09750).

### Inferences
- The real-world project's ~55% sign-only directional match is consistent with — and on the higher end of — what rigorous, leakage-controlled academic studies report for individual stocks and indices on short horizons (most cluster 50–66%).
- Papers reporting accuracy above ~70% on direction prediction, without a matching real trading P&L result or explicit leakage controls, should be treated skeptically; several papers in this range (Strathmore thesis, the unnamed 90–100% survey entry) lack the methodological disclosure needed to trust the number.
- "Accuracy" is inconsistently defined across papers (some are not clearly distinguishing directional hit rate from a normalized price-error metric), which is itself a methodological weakness worth noting when citing any of these figures.

### Gaps
- Could not extract the exact headline directional accuracy percentage from the original Fischer & Krauss (2018) paper itself (PDF was not text-extractable via the fetch tool); only its annualized-return figures and later critiques were confirmed.
- Could not confirm the specific "51.99%" logistic-regression figure's paper of origin; it may belong to a different study than Becker & Leschinski (2018).
- Did not independently verify the Strathmore thesis's 95%/96% LSTM accuracy methodology (train/test split, leakage checks) — flagged as suspect but not disproven.

## What methodologies do the best-performing (and most credible) studies use?

### Takeaway
The best-performing, best-documented studies across the literature use LSTM/deep-learning ensembles (often combined with Random Forest), with transformers used increasingly since ~2023; classical baselines (logistic regression, ARIMA) are frequently competitive with or superior to deep learning on point-forecast error even when deep learning wins narrowly on directional accuracy.

### Cited Findings
- Fischer & Krauss (2018) used **LSTM networks** on S&P 500 constituent out-of-sample directional movement, 1992–2015, with survivorship-bias-free data — [working paper](https://iwf.rw.fau.de/files/2015/12/11-2017.pdf).
- Zhong & Hitchcock combined **Random Forest and LSTM** for S&P 500 index and individual-stock direction prediction, incorporating technical, fundamental, and text data — [arXiv 2108.10826](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.10826).
- Al Ridhawi et al. (2026) used a **Node Transformer architecture integrated with BERT sentiment analysis**: reported 65% one-day directional accuracy vs. 58% for a plain LSTM baseline and 62% for a plain transformer, against a 50% random baseline, with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals reported — [arXiv 2603.05917](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.05917).
- Coolsaet et al. pre-trained **transformers on the TSX index** then fine-tuned on individual stocks; pre-training lowered binary cross-entropy from 0.69 to 0.64, but the authors note **XGBoost and an ensemble earned higher average daily returns than the fine-tuned transformer** — a case where a "better" loss metric did not translate into better trading performance — [arXiv 2605.23962](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/arxpapers/2605.23962.htm).
- Kesri & Salhi directly compared **LSTM vs. ARIMA** with walk-forward validation on Tesla: ARIMA won on point-forecast error (RMSE/MAE); LSTM had a marginal directional-accuracy edge (50.60% vs. 47.62%) — [MEST Journal](https://www.meste.org/ojs/index.php/mest/article/view/1575).
- Qian (2024) compared **LSTM vs. Random Forest** on S&P 500 and CSI 300 (2021–2024): LSTM outperformed RF on CSI 300, RF outperformed LSTM on S&P 500 — no single architecture dominates across markets — [found via search; exact venue/URL not confirmed].
- Kim, Muhn & Nikolaev tested **GPT-4 with chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting** for earnings-direction prediction: CoT prompting raised accuracy from ~52.33% (simple prompt) to 60.35%; a specialized neural network scored 60.4% (comparable to GPT-4 CoT); combining GPT-4 and the neural network reached ~63.2% — [SUERF Policy Brief 1008](https://suerf.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/SUERF-Policy-Brief-1008_Kim-et-al.pdf).
- Lopez-Lira & Tang used **zero-shot GPT-4** (no fine-tuning) prompted only with news headlines at temperature 0, evaluated strictly on data after the model's knowledge cutoff to avoid look-ahead bias — [arXiv 2304.07619](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07619v6).

### Inferences
- No single architecture (LSTM, transformer, RF, ARIMA, LLM) consistently dominates across studies; relative performance depends heavily on asset, market, and horizon, and "wins" are often narrow (a few percentage points).
- Ensembling (RF+LSTM, or LLM+specialized model) tends to produce the best-documented modest improvements over any single model.
- Several of the most rigorous studies (Coolsaet et al.; Kesri & Salhi) explicitly report cases where a model with better statistical loss or "accuracy" did NOT win on actual trading returns — a useful parallel for interpreting the real-world project's own accuracy-vs-return-error framing.

### Gaps
- Could not fully verify exact architecture details or hyperparameters of several cited studies (Qian 2024, the EAI 2026 paper) beyond what search snippets provided.

## Are there studies reporting 70-90%+ accuracy? Do they disclose methodology that explains the high numbers, or do they contradict it with a real trading result?

### Takeaway
Yes — several studies report accuracy in the 70–90%+ range, and in every case found, either (a) the paper is a thesis/preprint lacking disclosed leakage controls or proper walk-forward validation, or (b) a very high "accuracy"-like figure applies only to a narrow, non-tradable measure (e.g., instantaneous market reaction to already-published news) that the same paper's own tradable-strategy results show collapsing to 55-58% hit rates and near-zero/negative profit after transaction costs.

### Cited Findings
- **Lopez-Lira & Tang (GPT-4, 2024)**, the clearest example of this exact pattern: GPT-4's hit rate for predicting the **initial market reaction** to news headlines was **93.3% (overnight news) and 88.8% (intraday news)** — the ~90% figure the paper's abstract highlights. But this measures the direction of the market's immediate reaction to news that has already been published, not a forward-looking price call. For the **actual subsequent price drift** (the tradable signal, next 1-2 trading days), the hit rate drops to **58% (overnight) and 55% (intraday)**, with daily returns of 0.34%/0.50% and Sharpe ratios of 2.97/2.63 — and the authors explicitly note the strategy becomes **unprofitable at a 20 bps round-trip transaction cost**, with Sharpe ratios declining across the sample from 6.54 (2021 Q4) to 1.22 (Jan–May 2024) — [arXiv 2304.07619](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07619v6). This is a rigorous paper (out-of-sample period chosen specifically after GPT-4's training cutoff to avoid look-ahead bias) but its headline ~90% number is routinely misread as "GPT-4 predicts stock direction with 90% accuracy" when it actually measures something much narrower (instant market reaction, not forward prediction).
- **Fischer & Krauss (2018)**: claimed average annualized return >200% before transaction costs (82% after) — not an accuracy percentage but an extraordinary return figure in the same spirit; a follow-up paper calls this "not maintainable in the long run" and shows performance of comparable models collapsing to <0.1% average daily return after 2008 — [working paper](https://iwf.rw.fau.de/files/2015/12/11-2017.pdf), critique via search.
- **Strathmore University thesis**: reports 95%/96% LSTM accuracy for S&P 500/NASDAQ index prediction. This is flagged as methodologically suspect — it's an unpublished thesis, no leakage/train-test-split disclosure was found, and the figure is a dramatic outlier versus every peer-reviewed or arXiv paper found in this research — [Strathmore repository](https://su-plus.strathmore.edu/items/c5835860-8dea-40e0-ace1-61d5ec471571).
- **Unnamed survey entry**: a forex/stock DL survey cites one paper reaching "90%-100%" accuracy using LSTM, without the survey snippet disclosing that paper's validation methodology — [arXiv 2103.09750](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.09750). Flagged as suspect pending verification of the underlying paper's methodology.
- **General literature critique**: a practitioner critique (fast.ai forum, informal but widely cited in ML-finance circles) states that claims of >90% accuracy on price prediction are a near-universal red flag for data leakage, commonly from normalizing/scaling using the full dataset (including test data) before splitting — [fast.ai forum](https://forums.fast.ai/t/98-7-accuracy-on-bitcoin-price-prediction/21943). A vendor ML glossary gives a generic illustrative example that a model appearing to reach "95% accuracy due to data leakage may actually perform closer to 70% in real-world scenarios" — [Graphite Note](https://graphite-note.com/data-leakage/) (illustrative, not a specific paper's finding).
- The standard academic reference for backtest-overfitting-driven inflated results is Bailey et al.'s work on "Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial Charlatanism," cited via a Stanford MediaX summary — [Stanford MediaX](https://mediax.stanford.edu/?p=7509).

### Inferences
- The single most important finding for comparison purposes: when a paper reports a high (80-90%+) headline accuracy figure AND also reports a real/simulated trading result, the trading result consistently reveals much lower real-world predictive value (Lopez-Lira & Tang's drop from 90% to 55-58%, with eventual unprofitability net of costs; Fischer & Krauss's returns "not maintainable" and collapsing post-2008).
- High accuracy figures from theses/unpublished sources without disclosed methodology (Strathmore 95-96%) should not be treated as credible benchmarks without independent replication.
- This strongly supports treating the real-world project's 35% exact 3-way match / 55% directional match / 10.5pp return error as plausible, real-world, non-inflated numbers — they fall in the same band as the *tradable, forward-looking* portions of the most rigorous studies found (e.g., Lopez-Lira & Tang's 55-58% tradable drift hit rate), rather than the inflated headline numbers that don't survive a real trading test.

### Gaps
- Did not independently verify the underlying paper behind the "90-100%" entry in the arXiv 2103.09750 survey table — could not confirm whether it discloses a proper walk-forward/out-of-sample split.
- Could not fully audit the Strathmore thesis's methodology (full text was not fetched in this pass) — flagged as suspect based on outlier status alone, not a confirmed methodological flaw.

## What is the realistic "ceiling" in the legitimate literature for this problem, and which specific papers establish that?

### Takeaway
The legitimate, out-of-sample literature converges on a realistic ceiling of roughly **55-66% directional accuracy** for 1-day-ahead stock/index direction prediction using the best documented ML approaches, with many rigorous studies landing closer to 50-55% (essentially a coin flip with a small, sometimes statistically significant edge); accuracy decays further, or gains that look large are often artifacts of base-rate effects or leakage, once methodology is tightened.

### Cited Findings
- Zhong & Hitchcock's **66.18%** (S&P 500 index) and **62.09%** (individual stocks) combining RF+LSTM represent close to the top of the credible range found in this research — [arXiv 2108.10826](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.10826).
- Al Ridhawi et al.'s transformer+BERT-sentiment model reaching **65%** one-day accuracy (with reported confidence intervals) is similarly near the top of the credible range — [arXiv 2603.05917](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.05917).
- Multiple studies on individual stocks/indices with walk-forward or clean out-of-sample design land at **50-52%** (Ouargla study 51.22%; Tesla/Kesri & Salhi 50.60%; CSI 300 group 51.74%) — essentially at the ceiling the random-walk/efficient-market view predicts — [DSpace](https://dspace.univ-ouargla.dz/jspui/handle/123456789/40981?mode=full), [MEST Journal](https://www.meste.org/ojs/index.php/mest/article/view/1575), [Atlantis Press](https://www.atlantis-press.com/proceedings/icssed-26/126026271).
- A 2026 arXiv paper's base-rate warning (model: 57.0% vs. naive base-rate: 59.9% at a 30-day horizon) demonstrates that even the "ceiling-looking" ~55-60% figures can be deceptive — i.e., a figure in that range might reflect nothing more than class imbalance (more up-days than down-days in a bull market) rather than genuine skill — [arXiv paper, exact ID unconfirmed via search snippet].
- Lopez-Lira & Tang's tradable drift hit rates of **55-58%** (GPT-4, next-day) represent one of the better-documented "real" ceilings for a forward-looking, out-of-cutoff-period prediction task, and even this modest edge disappears net of a 20bps transaction cost — [arXiv 2304.07619](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07619v6).
- Malkiel's 2003 *Journal of Economic Perspectives* survey ("The Efficient Market Hypothesis and Its Critics") argues markets are "more efficient and less predictable than many recent academic papers would have us believe," while acknowledging some short-run momentum predictability exists — framing the theoretical ceiling as low and anomaly-dependent — [Princeton working paper PDF](https://swh.princeton.edu/~ceps/workingpapers/91malkiel.pdf).
- Eom, Oh & Jung (2007) found that **less efficient markets have higher directional prediction power** than more efficient ones across a sample of 27 markets — implying the realistic ceiling is market-dependent rather than a single fixed number, and that highly liquid, heavily-analyzed markets (S&P 500 large caps) should be expected to sit at the lower end of any observed range — [cited via search result; exact arXiv/journal ID not independently confirmed in this pass].
- Becker & Leschinski (2018) argue some genuine directional predictability exists in daily Dow Jones returns using simple factors and careful out-of-sample model selection, but that "simple forecasting methods have outperformed more complex machine learning" — suggesting the ceiling is reached by careful methodology, not model complexity — [Macrosynergy summary](https://macrosynergy.com/?p=40742); [RePEc dp-624](https://ideas.repec.org/p/han/dpaper/dp-624.html).

### Inferences
- Taken together, the specific papers above (Zhong & Hitchcock 66.18%/62.09%; Al Ridhawi et al. 65%; Lopez-Lira & Tang's tradable 55-58%) define a realistic literature ceiling of roughly **60-66%** for the single best, most carefully-evaluated individual studies on 1-day-ahead index/stock direction, with the broader body of rigorous studies clustering at **50-55%**.
- The real-world project's **~55% directional (sign-only) match** sits almost exactly at the median/typical value reported by the most careful academic studies (e.g., Lopez-Lira & Tang's 55-58% tradable drift hit rate; CSI 300 51.74%; Ouargla 51.22%), and is not far below the best documented results in the literature (Zhong & Hitchcock 62-66%; Al Ridhawi 65%). This is a meaningful, citable comparison point: the project's result is in line with legitimate academic benchmarks, not an outlier below them.
- The project's ~35% exact 3-way decision match (buy/sell/hold, presumably against a majority-class or random baseline of roughly 33%) should similarly be read against a near-chance baseline; no paper found in this research reports a directly comparable 3-way decision-match benchmark, which is a genuine gap (see below).

### Gaps
- No paper found in this research reports a directly comparable "exact 3-way decision match" (buy/sell/hold or similar categorical) accuracy figure analogous to the project's 35% metric; all academic comparisons found use binary up/down direction, continuous return error, or portfolio hit-rate framings. This is a structural gap in the literature relative to the project's specific evaluation design, not just a search gap.
- No paper found reports a return-magnitude error metric directly comparable to the project's "~10.5 percentage-point average return error" (most papers report RMSE/MAE in price-level or percentage-return terms using different scales, not a directly comparable "average error in predicted return vs. actual return in percentage points"), making a precise numeric comparison on that specific metric not possible from the sources gathered.
- Could not verify Eom, Oh & Jung (2007)'s exact publication venue/DOI in this pass — cite with that caveat.
