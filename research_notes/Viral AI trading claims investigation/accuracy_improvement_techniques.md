# Accuracy-Improvement Techniques for LLM-Based Stock Prediction Systems

## Key Question 1: Debate/adversarial reasoning (Bull vs Bear) vs simple independent-vote ensembling — is there a measured accuracy delta?

### Takeaway
There is no controlled, apples-to-apples experiment anywhere found that isolates the Bull/Bear debate mechanism's contribution vs. a non-debate baseline inside TradingAgents itself — the paper's own headline numbers are return/Sharpe/drawdown comparisons against rule-based strategies (MACD, KDJ&RSI, SMA, Buy&Hold), not against a non-debate LLM baseline, and it contains no ablation removing debate. Adjacent 2025-2026 preprints suggest debate-style multi-agent reasoning *can* beat simple baselines, but the evidence is thin, mostly unreplicated preprints, and in the one directly relevant study on ensemble aggregation methods, plain majority voting over diverse models performed no better than a single model — the aggregation method (not diversity of framing) was what mattered.

### Cited Findings
- TradingAgents (arXiv 2412.20138, v6) reports cumulative return (CR), annualized return (AR), Sharpe ratio (SR), and max drawdown (MDD) for 3 stocks (AAPL, GOOGL, AMZN) against Buy&Hold, MACD, KDJ&RSI, ZMR, and SMA baselines — NOT against a non-debate LLM baseline. Example: AAPL — TradingAgents CR 26.62% / SR 8.21 vs. best rule-based baseline (KDJ&RSI) CR 2.05% / SR 1.64. — [arXiv 2412.20138v6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20138v6)
- The TradingAgents paper contains **no ablation study** that disables or removes the Bull/Bear debate and compares performance with vs. without it; claims that debate improves performance are qualitative/discussion-section claims, not backed by a controlled comparison in the paper. — [arXiv 2412.20138 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.20138v6)
- The paper itself flags its own result as suspiciously high: a footnote attributes the AAPL Sharpe ratio of 8.21 to "few pullbacks during a three-month test window," and the text claims drawdown was kept under 2% while the reported AMZN MDD is actually 2.11% — i.e., the paper's own numbers contain internal inconsistencies. — [arXiv 2412.20138v6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20138v6)
- A November 2025 preprint modeling FOMC-style deliberation with hawkish/dovish-belief LLM agents (debate-based) reports the debate approach "significantly outperforms standard LLM-based baselines in prediction accuracy" for monetary-policy decision classification (accepted at PRIMA 2025). No exact percentage was retrievable from search snippets. — [arXiv 2511.02469](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02469)
- A separate (venue/peer-review status unconfirmed) study used a dual-agent debate (baseline forecast + challenger, up to 3 rounds) across 75 financial market indicators in 5 asset classes; the debate-consensus forecast was reported as more accurate and directionally stable than the single-agent baseline, with the benefit concentrated in volatile assets like crypto. — found via search, venue unconfirmed.
- FinDebate (accepted FinNLP@EMNLP 2025) uses 5 specialized debating agents with retrieval; its reported gains are about **calibrated confidence and analysis quality**, not a direct forecasting-accuracy benchmark — it explicitly adds a "safe debate protocol" to counter debate-induced overconfidence. — [FinDebate, FinNLP@EMNLP 2025](https://aitopics.org/doc/arxivorg:9B7AC9B6)
- A corporate credit-risk study found structured multi-agent debate improved analytical rigor and perceived usefulness but increased computation time — again an analysis-quality claim, not a measured accuracy delta.
- Directly relevant to aggregation-method choice: "When Agents Disagree: The Selection Bottleneck" (arXiv 2603.20324) ran 42 tasks (N=210) comparing judge-based selection, majority vote, and MoA-style synthesis over a diverse 3-model team (Claude Opus, GPT-5.4, Gemini 2.5 Pro) against a single-Opus baseline, using Bradley-Terry win rates (0.500 = parity): **judge-selection BT-WR = 0.810**; **majority vote BT-WR = 0.496** (statistically indistinguishable from a single model); **MoA synthesis BT-WR = 0.179** (actively worse than baseline). Judge beat vote by Δ=+0.313 (Hedges' g=1.61) and beat synthesis by Δ=+0.631 (g=3.86, judge won on all 42/42 tasks). A homogeneous 3x-same-model team with judge selection scored 0.512 — the diversity benefit (Δ=+0.298) appeared *only* when paired with judge-based selection; under majority voting, diversity was "inert" and performance was chance-level even with a diverse team. A decoupled 2-judge-panel check attenuated win rates by 53-67% but preserved rank order (Spearman ρ=0.90). — [alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.20324](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.20324.md)
- Self-consistency's originating paper (Wang et al. 2022) found that an ensemble of *different* models performed significantly worse than self-consistency sampling (many samples of *one* model, majority vote), partly because a weaker ensemble member drags the result down. — [arXiv 2203.11171](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.11171)
- A 2025 "consortium voting" paper (arXiv 2510.19507) pooling up to 15 different LLMs found multi-model voting "performs well" only "under reasonable constraints in consortium composition" — i.e., diverse-model voting is conditional on how the team is composed, not a guaranteed win.

### Inferences
- The strongest, most rigorous finding retrieved (arXiv 2603.20324) directly undercuts the premise that simply adding model/framing diversity under majority voting improves outcomes — it found diverse-team majority voting statistically equivalent to a single model, and that the aggregation method (judge/arbiter selection) mattered far more than diversity itself. This is a significant, actionable signal for the system in question: if its 3-parallel-call majority-vote architecture behaves like the paper's "vote" condition, switching to a judge/arbiter-style aggregation (an LLM or rule selecting the best of the 3 outputs rather than counting votes) may be a higher-leverage change than adopting Bull/Bear debate.
- No source found provides a clean, controlled "debate vs. independent-vote" accuracy delta specific to stock direction/decision prediction. TradingAgents' own numbers cannot be used to credit the debate mechanism specifically, since there's no non-debate LLM control arm in the paper.

### Gaps
- No ablation isolating the Bull/Bear debate's marginal contribution exists in the TradingAgents paper or in any source found.
- No study was found that directly compares "N independent parallel LLM calls with majority vote" (the current system's architecture) against "multi-round Bull/Bear debate" head-to-head on the same stock-direction prediction task with disclosed accuracy numbers for both arms.
- The FOMC-debate paper's exact accuracy percentage and the 75-indicator debate study's venue/peer-review status could not be confirmed from search snippets; both should be treated as suggestive, not proven.

## Key Question 2: Segment-specific/specialized models (by volatility regime or sector) vs one-size-fits-all

### Takeaway
There is real, reasonably strong evidence that regime- and sector-aware specialization helps, but the single most rigorous, longest-horizon study found that a **hybrid** of generalist + specialist outperformed either pure approach alone — i.e., the win isn't "always segment," it's "blend segment-specific and general information."

### Cited Findings
- Hanauer et al. compared a generalist stock-return model, per-industry specialist models, and a hybrid combining both, on U.S. stock returns from 1957-2023 (long out-of-sample window, international robustness checks). The **hybrid model outperformed the pure specialist model** out-of-sample, with higher Sharpe ratios and lower risk than both the pure generalist and pure specialist alternatives. — [Quantpedia summary, Hanauer et al.](https://quantpedia.com/are-sector-specific-machine-learning-models-better-than-generalists/)
- A 2026 arXiv paper on regime-aware in-context learning for volatility forecasting reports its best regime-aware variant reduced high-volatility-period forecasting error by approximately 27% vs. GJR-GARCH, the strongest classical (non-regime-aware) baseline in that regime. — [arXiv 2603.10299](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.10299)
- A 2025 arXiv study on S&P 500 volatility trains separate HAR models per volatility-cluster "regime" and uses an XGBoost classifier to assign regime probability at inference time, rather than one global HAR model. — [arXiv 2510.03236](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03236v1)
- Counter-evidence: Nybo (2021) compared GARCH vs. ANN volatility forecasts split by sector volatility profile and found GARCH outperformed ANN in at least one low-volatility sector grouping — specialization/model choice did not uniformly favor the more "sophisticated" model; the right model depends on the regime.
- A 2026 paper on regime-aware transformers found the most predictable stocks were defensive names with stable revenue, *regardless of sector label* — suggesting fundamental business stability (not sector taxonomy itself) drives predictability differences. This is directly relevant to the background finding that broad-index ETFs predict much better (81%) than narrative-driven single stocks like TSLA: it implies the "ETF vs. single stock" gap may be better explained by a stability/narrative-dependence axis than a sector axis.

### Inferences
- For a system already showing an 81% vs. ~54% accuracy gap between broad ETFs and narrative stocks like TSLA, the Hanauer et al. hybrid result and the "defensive/stable vs. narrative-driven" predictability finding both suggest segmenting the ML/LLM approach along a volatility- or narrative-dependence axis (not necessarily sector) is more likely to pay off than sector-based segmentation per se. A pure "TSLA-specific model" risk is overfitting to a regime that will drift; the hybrid design (blend a specialist adjustment on top of a generalist base) has the best empirical support found.

### Gaps
- No source found applies regime/sector specialization specifically to an LLM-based (vs. classical ML/GARCH) prediction system, so transferability to a Gemini-consensus architecture is inferred, not directly evidenced.
- Exact accuracy-percentage deltas (vs. Sharpe/error-reduction deltas) for sector-specific vs. generalist classification accuracy were not found.

## Key Question 3: Prompt-engineering techniques (structured reasoning, uncertainty quantification, self-consistency, calibration) with disclosed before/after numbers

### Takeaway
The best-documented, most concrete number found is Kim/Muhn/Nikolaev's GPT-4 chain-of-thought earnings-direction prediction result (~60.4% vs. human analysts' 52.7%), but evidence that CoT specifically (vs. other prompting choices) caused that lift is incomplete. Self-consistency (many samples of one model, majority vote) has strong, well-quantified gains on general reasoning benchmarks (+17.9pp on GSM8K) but no dedicated stock-prediction study was found; calibration research consistently shows LLMs are overconfident and that reasoning-enhanced models do NOT fix calibration even when accuracy is fine.

### Cited Findings
- Kim, Muhn, and Nikolaev: GPT-4 given only balance sheet + income statement data (no narrative context), prompted with chain-of-thought reasoning, reached ~60.4% accuracy predicting earnings direction (rise/fall); professional human analysts predicting one month after the same financial statements were released achieved 52.7% accuracy. — reported via multiple secondary summaries (exact primary citation: Kim, Muhn & Nikolaev, "Financial Statement Analysis with Large Language Models"); note the paper has also been described as an early/unreviewed draft in some coverage, so treat the 60.4% figure as not yet independently replicated. — [PromptLayer summary](https://www.promptlayer.com/research-papers/financial-statement-analysis-with-large-language-models)
- The exact "simple prompt" (non-CoT) baseline accuracy for the same GPT-4/earnings task could not be confirmed — sources disagree, with one saying a simple prompt "performs similarly" to CoT, which would undercut attributing the 60.4% figure specifically to chain-of-thought.
- VISTA (arXiv 2505.18570) found chain-of-thought's effect on stock-related prediction is model-dependent: for Deepseek-vl2, CoT reduced MSE on one company (Capgemini) from 0.0083 to 0.0054 (a 34.94% reduction), but for LLaVA, CoT prompting *increased* MSE on all four companies tested. — [arXiv 2505.18570](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.18570)
- A preprint survey claims "advanced prompting" can improve financial decision-making accuracy by 18-42% vs. standard approaches, but this is an aggregated literature claim that could not be traced to specific verifiable underlying studies — treat as unverified.
- Original self-consistency paper (Wang et al. 2022): on GSM8K with PaLM-540B, greedy decoding (single sample) scored 56.5% vs. self-consistency (multiple sampled reasoning paths + majority vote) scoring 74.4% — a **+17.9 percentage point gain**. Gains were reported to saturate around N=30-40 samples, with N=10-20 capturing most of the benefit. — [arXiv 2203.11171](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.11171)
- A 2025 preprint ("Self-Consistency Is Losing Its Edge") argues the technique's cost-benefit has worsened on modern (post-2022) models, since token costs scale roughly linearly with sample count while the accuracy gain shrinks on stronger base models; it recommends reserving multi-sample self-consistency for problems that demonstrably exceed a given model's single-pass reliability, rather than applying it indiscriminately. — [arXiv 2511.00751](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.00751)
- No study was found that tests self-consistency sampling specifically on stock-direction prediction; the CNMV working paper (No. 96, March 2026, Crisóstomo & Mykhalyuk) tested ChatGPT/Gemini/DeepSeek/Perplexity across 3 prompting strategies (basic, structured, chain-of-thought) for stock investing decisions and found models "can beat the market when guided and supervised" but exhibited "recurring reasoning failures... financial misconceptions and reliance on outdated or hallucinated information," requiring "substantial human oversight." I was unable to extract the exact per-platform/per-prompt accuracy numbers from Tables 4-5 (PDF was not machine-readable via available tools), so these specific figures are a **gap**, not a confirmed finding. — [CNMV Working Paper 96](https://internet.cnmv.es/DocPortal/Publicaciones/MONOGRAFIAS/DT_96_Large_Language_Modelsen.pdf) / [arXiv 2603.19944](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19944)
- The CNMV paper reports that grounding recommendations in official regulatory filings appears to improve forecasting accuracy (specific number not retrieved).
- Calibration research: "Do LLMs Know What They Don't Know?" (arXiv 2512.16030) tested models on Kalshi prediction-market questions and found accuracy varied only modestly across models while calibration error (ECE) varied roughly 3x across models; critically, **reasoning-enhanced models did not improve calibration**, and models averaged a 27% error rate even when expressing >90% confidence. This is prediction-market forecasting, not equity/macro forecasting directly, so transfers only partially. — [arXiv 2512.16030](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.16030v1)
- FinBench (arXiv 2607.16229) and FinAbstain (arXiv 2607.24875), both 2026 preprints on calibration/uncertainty for agentic financial forecasting, have only pilot-scale or simulated results (FinBench: 1 trading day, 3 tickers; FinAbstain: simulated/illustrative table, not empirical) — neither offers real, validated before/after accuracy numbers yet.
- A vendor case study (Turing) on a 10,000+-prompt chain-of-thought finance dataset reports an expected 4-6 percentage-point accuracy lift on fine-tuned benchmarks — a company claim, not independently verified or peer-reviewed. — [Turing case study](https://www.turing.com/case-study/improving-accuracy-and-reducing-hallucinations-with-finance-cot-prompts)

### Inferences
- The clearest actionable, well-evidenced prompting lever is self-consistency/majority-vote over many samples of a single strong model — but it comes with the caveat (2025 preprint) that its marginal value has shrunk on today's stronger models, so it should be tested/measured on the Gemini models in use rather than assumed.
- The calibration literature's consistent finding that reasoning ability and calibration are *decoupled* is directly relevant: adding more elaborate reasoning chains to the Gemini consensus engine should not be expected to fix overconfidence/miscalibration without an explicit calibration step (e.g., post-hoc calibration mapping, which the system's existing `confidence_calibration_map.json`/`calibration_profile_builder.py` suggests is already attempted — validating that approach against the Kalshi-style finding that raw confidence is unreliable is worthwhile).

### Gaps
- No dedicated, verifiable before/after accuracy number for self-consistency sampling applied to stock direction/decision prediction specifically.
- Could not extract CNMV Working Paper 96's actual Table 4/5 per-platform, per-prompt-strategy accuracy percentages (the PDF text was not extractable via available tools) — this is the single most directly relevant paper to the key question and should be manually retrieved and read if feasible.
- No controlled study found that isolates "explicit uncertainty quantification in the prompt" as a standalone lever with before/after numbers for financial prediction (only general-domain Kalshi calibration results).

## Key Question 4: Hybrid LLM qualitative reasoning + traditional ML (gradient boosting etc.) ensemble — real reported accuracy improvements

### Takeaway
There is solid evidence that hybrid ensembles of tree-based models (LightGBM/CatBoost) with deep sequence models (LSTM) outperform individual models by double digits in several 2025 preprints, but **no study was found that ensembles an LLM's qualitative output with a gradient-boosting model** — this specific combination (which is what the user's system would need) remains unvalidated in the literature retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- arXiv 2505.23084 (May 2025): a stacked ensemble of LSTM + LightGBM + CatBoost, combined via a two-layer LSTM meta-learner, reported **10-15% accuracy improvement** over individual component models; one reviewer summary cites R²=0.8152 for the ensemble vs. R²=0.7882 for the best single model (CatBoost). This is a single preprint without independent replication or peer review confirmed. — [arXiv 2505.23084](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23084)
- Oukhouya et al. (2023): an XGBoost + LSTM hybrid was reported to outperform single models (no specific percentage retrieved).
- A 2025 study (Kayit & Ismail) concludes ensembles generally outperform standalone and hybrid models in stock price prediction tasks (general conclusion, not a specific number).
- A FedCSIS 2024 sector stock-trend prediction entry reported the lowest weighted error (0.5941) among competitors in a preliminary phase, but its final-phase result was weaker — illustrating that preliminary-leaderboard wins for hybrid/ensemble models don't always hold up out-of-sample.

### Inferences
- The absence of any LLM+gradient-boosting ensemble study in the literature searched is itself a notable finding: this specific hybrid architecture (qualitative LLM signal + quantitative GBM signal, blended) appears to be a genuine white space rather than a well-trodden, validated technique. Any claim that "LLM + XGBoost ensembling improves accuracy by X%" would currently be unsupported by direct evidence; the closest analogue (deep-learning + tree-ensemble stacking) shows the *general pattern* that heterogeneous-model stacking can yield double-digit gains, which is suggestive but not proof for the LLM case.

### Gaps
- No source found directly tests "LLM directional call + gradient-boosted-tree quantitative model" as ensemble members for stock prediction. This should be flagged explicitly as unproven if the user's team considers it, despite being a very natural extension.
- No accuracy numbers (vs. R²/error metrics) were found for any of the hybrid studies in Key Question 4, limiting direct comparability to the system's classification-accuracy framing (34% exact 3-way, 54% directional).

## Key Question 5: Additional alternative data sources with measured accuracy contributions

### Takeaway
Credible, traceable academic evidence exists for satellite/ports data (strong, multi-country) and for insider-sale-at-a-loss signals (real but modest and return-based, not accuracy-based); options-flow/dark-pool data and social-media sentiment show real but small, conditional, and often vendor-exaggerated effects, with one rigorous live test showing social sentiment degrading badly out of sample.

### Cited Findings
- **Satellite data — ports (strongest evidence):** Yu et al. (2023, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*, Nature-published) found satellite-based container counts at ports significantly predicted daily stock-index returns in 27 of 33 countries studied (2019-2021). — [Nature/HSSC](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-023-01891-9.pdf)
- **Satellite data — retail parking lots:** An AFA-linked working paper found that the introduction of satellite parking-lot car-count data reduced active mutual funds' stock-picking ability in covered stocks — implying the signal is real and gets quickly absorbed/arbitraged away once available (an "alpha decay" pattern relevant to any team considering adopting this data source today). — [AFA working paper](https://afajof.org/management/viewp.php?n=83300)
- A frequently-cited "18% improvement in earnings estimates from satellite imagery" and an "85% accuracy, MIT Sloan" figure both appeared in blog coverage but could **not be traced to a verifiable primary source** — explicitly flag these as unverified if encountered elsewhere.
- **Options flow / dark pool data:** No peer-reviewed, rigorously verified accuracy figure was found. Vendor claims (Quanta AI: ~58-62% five-day directional accuracy when dark-pool blocks align with same-side options flow, dropping to near-random for dark-pool prints alone; TradeAlgo: 61-68%, citing an unconfirmed secondhand FINRA reference) are unverified marketing claims. The one independent, if informal, backtest found (a random-forest model using dark-pool indicators to predict S&P 500 direction) got only a **52.56% hit ratio** — barely above a coin flip, and not peer-reviewed. — [Substack backtest](https://abouttrading.substack.com/p/cracking-the-dark-pool-forecasting)
- **Insider trading signals:** Peter Kelly (*Review of Financial Studies*) found only a *subset* of insider sales — specifically sales made at a loss — predict future returns; sales made at a gain showed "essentially no return predictability." This is a statistically-significant abnormal-return finding, not a directional-accuracy/hit-rate figure. — [Tuck/Dartmouth summary](https://tuck.dartmouth.edu/uploads/content/insider_sentiment_stock_returns.pdf)
- Jeng, Metrick & Zeckhauser: insider *purchases* were more informative than sales; their sales-based portfolio showed no statistically/economically significant abnormal returns.
- Li, Wang, Yan & Zhang (*Journal of Portfolio Management*, 2019): insider-trade predictability is conditional — stronger when the stock price is far from its reference "anchor" level.
- An options-market study found insider-trading-pattern predictability *declines* as options-market sentiment turns negative, and is stronger when stock-specific information asymmetry is high — i.e., the signal is regime-conditional, not constant.
- An older estimate (lower confidence, flagged as such by the source) put insider-trading-based strategy profits at a modest ~3% per annum after 2% transaction costs — i.e., even where the signal is real, the economically exploitable edge is thin once realistic costs are applied.
- **Social media sentiment:** A TSLA-specific study combining FinBERT sentiment + LSTM reported 70.6% training accuracy but only **46% accuracy in a 63-trading-day live paper-trading simulation** — i.e., it failed to beat a coin flip out of sample, and the authors concluded "raw daily sentiment aggregation from Twitter alone is insufficient for reliable stock price movement prediction." This is especially notable since TSLA is explicitly called out in the user's own system as a weak spot for narrative-driven single stocks — this result reinforces that sentiment-only approaches for TSLA-like names are fragile. — found via search (academic preprint on TSLA Twitter/X sentiment + LSTM).
- A 2025 hybrid sentiment+market-data model reported a **21.6% average accuracy improvement for high-discourse stocks**, but "limited gains and even decreased accuracy for stocks with low social-media engagement" — meaning the value of social sentiment is conditional on a stock's social-media volume, not universal.
- A 2024 PeerJ study and a 2025 study both note social-media-sentiment predictive accuracy is inconsistent across sectors/companies, calling for broader validation.
- Several higher accuracy claims (75%+, 85-94%) exist in the literature but come from sentiment-classification tasks (classifying text sentiment), not from stock-direction prediction tasks — these should not be conflated.

### Inferences
- The social-media-sentiment "21.6% improvement for high-discourse stocks, limited/negative for low-discourse stocks" finding, combined with the TSLA live-test failure (46% out-of-sample), is a notable, directly relevant pattern: it suggests sentiment data is more useful for highly-discussed (often meme/narrative) stocks in theory, yet the one live TSLA-specific test actually performed worse than chance — implying that *how* sentiment is aggregated/used matters enormously, and naive daily-sentiment-aggregation approaches are unlikely to fix the system's weak TSLA performance without more sophisticated methodology (e.g., event-triggered sentiment, sentiment divergence/shock detection, rather than raw daily aggregate).
- Options-flow/dark-pool data has a plausible but currently evidence-thin case; the one independent backtest found (52.56%) suggests current vendor claims of 58-68% accuracy should be treated skeptically until independently replicated.

### Gaps
- No rigorous, peer-reviewed accuracy/hit-rate figure (as opposed to vendor marketing) was found for options-flow or dark-pool data; this should be stated explicitly as an evidence gap, not treated as proven.
- The "18% earnings estimate improvement" and "85% MIT Sloan accuracy" satellite-data claims could not be verified and should not be cited without locating the primary source.
- No study found directly benchmarks insider-trading signals as a *directional accuracy* contribution (vs. abnormal-return framing) comparable to the user's 34%/54% metrics.

## Key Question 6: 2025-2026 research on reducing LLM hallucination/inconsistency in financial reasoning relevant to majority-vote consensus

### Takeaway
The most concrete, quantified 2026 result found is a neuro-symbolic approach (pairing LLM reasoning with deterministic code execution + a verifier model) that compressed hallucination rates to ~1.2%, far better than pure-LLM baselines, but it is a single unreplicated preprint. Broader 2025-2026 literature converges on: ground answers in verifiable data/retrieval, execute numeric calculations in code rather than LLM text generation, train/prompt models to explicitly refuse when data is missing, and recognize that reasoning ability and calibration/hallucination-resistance are measured separately — none of these specifically targets improving a majority-vote consensus system's reliability, but all are directly applicable to it.

### Cited Findings
- VeNRA (arXiv 2603.04663, March 2026 preprint): pairs LLMs with deterministic Python execution and a small verifier model (neuro-symbolic routing); reports this combination compresses systemic hallucination rates to **~1.2%**. The paper also critiques current hallucination benchmarks for relying almost exclusively on "Generative Noise" (prompting a model to invent an error) rather than realistic failure modes, and instead builds training/eval data by deliberately sabotaging real financial records. Code is public on GitHub. Single preprint, unreplicated. — [alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.04663](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.04663)
- Pesaranghader & Li (January 2026 survey): categorizes hallucination causes into model/data/context factors, and pairs detection methods (uncertainty estimation, reasoning-consistency checks) with mitigations (knowledge grounding, confidence calibration) — a structured taxonomy rather than a single quantified result.
- A January 2026 paper on small language models (SLMs) for financial classification found SLMs are *more* prone to factual hallucination in reasoning and show weaker classification performance than larger models, proposing a "hallucination-aware reasoning" method as a countermeasure — relevant if the system ever considers smaller/cheaper models to cut cost, since the finding implies smaller models carry a real reliability penalty specifically in finance tasks.
- JurisTech's 2026 practitioner benchmark on financial hallucination found that prompt wording, workflow design, and test conditions determine whether a model refuses unsupported answers or fabricates a plausible-sounding one; it also found the *same* model ranked very differently across two separate benchmarks — implying public hallucination leaderboard scores may not predict behavior in a specific financial workflow, so in-house testing on the system's own prompts/data is necessary rather than relying on published model rankings.
- Turing's finance chain-of-thought dataset case study (vendor claim, not peer-reviewed) reports an expected 4-6 percentage-point accuracy lift from fine-tuning on 10,000+ multi-hop finance CoT prompts designed partly to reduce hallucination.
- Directly tying back to Key Question 1/3: the Kalshi-based calibration study (arXiv 2512.16030) found reasoning-enhanced models did NOT have better calibration despite comparable or better raw accuracy — i.e., more elaborate reasoning (such as a debate architecture might produce) is not itself a hallucination/calibration fix.

### Inferences
- For a majority-vote consensus system specifically, the most load-bearing, literature-consistent takeaway is: majority voting over LLM outputs does not, by itself, address hallucination if the errors across the 3 parallel calls are correlated (e.g., all 3 calls share the same underlying training-data blind spot or the same tendency to fabricate a plausible-sounding technical justification). None of the sources found directly tested whether the specific hallucination-reduction techniques (code-execution grounding, refusal training) reduce *correlated* error across ensemble members, which is the key unknown for a majority-vote system's reliability.
- Grounding numeric/factual claims in verifiable retrieved data or deterministic code execution (rather than having the LLM "reason" in free text about numbers) is the most consistently recommended, evidence-backed lever across multiple 2026 sources, and is a concrete, implementable change distinct from "ensembling" or "debate."

### Gaps
- No study found specifically measures whether hallucination-reduction techniques reduce *correlated* error across multiple parallel LLM calls in a majority-vote ensemble (as opposed to single-call accuracy) — this is the most directly relevant open question for the system in question and remains unanswered in the literature retrieved.
- The VeNRA 1.2% hallucination-rate figure is from a single unreplicated preprint and should not be treated as an established, generalizable number.

## Cross-cutting notes for the report writer

- **Overall evidentiary quality is weak-to-moderate across nearly all six questions.** A large fraction of the strongest-sounding numbers come from single, unreplicated 2025-2026 preprints (many from arXiv's newer/less-reviewed subject areas) or from vendor marketing material with no disclosed methodology. Treat any single-source numeric claim in this note as provisional.
- **The single most rigorous, best-designed study found across all six questions is "When Agents Disagree: The Selection Bottleneck" (arXiv 2603.20324)** — it directly addresses the core architectural question (does ensembling under majority voting beat a single model?) with a reasonably controlled design (42 tasks, N=210, decoupled-judge robustness check) and found majority voting over a diverse team was statistically equivalent to a single model, while judge-based selection was substantially better. This has direct, actionable implications for the system's current 3-parallel-call-majority-vote architecture, independent of whether Bull/Bear debate is ever adopted.
- **No source anywhere found provides a controlled, same-task comparison of "debate" vs. "independent parallel voting" for stock direction prediction specifically** — this remains a genuine open question that would require the user's own A/B validation, exactly as the user's background notes already acknowledge.
- **The TSLA-weakness / narrative-stock-weakness pattern in the user's own system is echoed in the literature**: predictability research (regime-aware transformer paper) attributes low predictability to narrative/non-defensive stocks regardless of sector, and sentiment research shows naive sentiment aggregation actually underperforms on a TSLA-like live test (46% out-of-sample) — suggesting that fixing the TSLA weak spot likely requires a fundamentally different signal-processing approach (event/shock-based rather than daily-aggregate sentiment, or explicit narrative/hype detection) rather than simply adding more alternative data sources of the same kind.
