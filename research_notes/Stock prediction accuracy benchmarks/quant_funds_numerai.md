# Quant Funds & Numerai: Disclosed Prediction-Accuracy / Win-Rate Figures

## Has Renaissance Technologies (Medallion), Two Sigma, Citadel, or D.E. Shaw ever disclosed a specific stock-direction prediction accuracy or hit-rate percentage?

### Takeaway
No major quant fund has officially, firm-sourced disclosed a per-prediction directional-accuracy or hit-rate number. The one figure that circulates publicly — "right 50.75% of the time" for Renaissance's Medallion fund — is a secondhand anecdote attributed to former co-CEO Robert Mercer in Gregory Zuckerman's book *The Man Who Solved the Market*, not an audited or firm-published statistic, and it describes trade-level win rate (not necessarily "stock direction prediction" in the sense of a discrete forecast). Two Sigma, Citadel, and D.E. Shaw have no identified public disclosure of any prediction-accuracy or hit-rate figure at all — only overall fund/strategy returns are known.

### Cited Findings
- The ~50.75% figure is attributed to Robert Mercer, who reportedly told a friend/colleague the fund was "right 50.75 percent of the time... but we're 100 percent right 50.75 percent of the time," making the point that a small, consistent statistical edge compounded over huge trade volume produces large profits — not that the fund has near-coin-flip-level "intelligence." This is relayed via Gregory Zuckerman's book *The Man Who Solved the Market* and summarized in reader notes — [Goodreads notes on The Man Who Solved the Market](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/44698571-the-man-who-solved-the-market/58027759-thomas-wolf), [Goodreads notes (Zubair Habib)](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/46025922-the-man-who-solved-the-market/13424516-zubair-habib/bd279716-8be3-44b0-849d-d5527dbb7cd6)
- A related, similarly-themed but distinct quote attributed to quant researcher Elwyn Berlekamp: "If you trade a lot, you only need to be right 51 percent of the time" — [Goodreads quotes page for The Man Who Solved the Market](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/68288870)
- A book-summary site (secondary, not primary) frames this as "roughly 50.75% accuracy that generated billions" — this is the summarizer's paraphrase, not a direct quote from the book or the firm — [Sumizeit summary](https://www.sumizeit.com/books/the-man-who-solved-the-market)
- Renaissance Technologies itself has never confirmed this figure or any other hit-rate statistic; the firm has consistently declined to discuss its methods publicly. UCLA finance professor Bradford Cornell, in his paper "Medallion Fund: The Ultimate Counterexample?", examined Medallion's reported average gross annual return of 66.1% (39.2% net) from 1988–2018, with standard deviation 31.7% gross (20.3% net), and concluded the returns are so large they "stretch to the limit" normal explanations of skill — but Cornell's analysis is based on return statistics, not on any disclosed accuracy/hit-rate number, and Renaissance declined to comment — [Institutional Investor: "Famed Medallion Fund Stretches Explanation to the Limit, Professor Claims"](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bswymr8cih3jeaslxc00/portfolio/famed-medallion-fund-stretches-explanation-to-the-limit-professor-claims)
- Duke finance professor Campbell Harvey, responding to Cornell's paper, speculated (without firm data) that Medallion's edge likely comes from many small, similarly profitable bets with very low execution costs, rather than high per-trade predictive accuracy — again speculation by an outside academic, not a disclosed fund statistic — [Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bswymr8cih3jeaslxc00/portfolio/famed-medallion-fund-stretches-explanation-to-the-limit-professor-claims)
- Bloomberg Businessweek's "The Unsolved Mystery of the Medallion Fund's Success" likewise treats the fund's methodology and any granular performance metrics beyond aggregate returns as undisclosed and essentially unknown outside the firm — [Bloomberg](https://brp-prod-bcc.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2019-11-12/the-unsolved-mystery-of-the-medallion-fund-s-success)
- Searches specifically for Two Sigma, Citadel, and D.E. Shaw executives disclosing a stock-prediction "accuracy" or "hit rate" percentage returned no results from any primary source (interviews, filings, academic collaborations, or leaks); the only "accuracy rate" figure that surfaced in searches tied to quant trading (an "81% prediction accuracy" claim from a firm called "Glasstx Capital") appeared in a promotional press release, not independent reporting, and is not relevant to the four funds in scope — [Barchart press release](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/32176644/exclusive-interview-with-glasstx-capitals-quantitative-team-how-the-gfc-quantitative-trading-system-achieved-a-breakthrough-81-percent-prediction-accuracy-rate) (flagged here only as a low-credibility result to exclude, not as evidence)
- General hedge-fund-disclosure research confirms a structural reason no such figures exist: an academic study of investor letters from 434 hedge funds found that better-performing funds systematically disclose *less* quantitative detail about performance and holdings, consistent with "proprietary cost" theory (revealing more helps competitors free-ride on the fund's research) — [Institutional Investor research summary / HU Berlin seminar paper](https://www.wiwi.hu-berlin.de/en/Professorships/bwl/finance/seminars/papers-fin-acc-res-seminars/16s/cassar.pdf)

### Inferences
- The absence of search results naming Two Sigma, Citadel, or D.E. Shaw in connection with any disclosed accuracy/hit-rate figure is itself informative: these firms are at least as secretive as Renaissance about methodology, and if such a figure existed it would likely be widely cited (as the Medallion 50.75% anecdote is). Its total absence from financial journalism, court filings, or academic literature for these three firms supports concluding no such figure has been made public.
- The ~50.75% figure, even if accurate, illustrates a general principle (small persistent edge + huge trade volume + strict risk control = large compounded profit) rather than functioning as a rigorous, audited "stock direction prediction accuracy" metric comparable to what a prediction-accuracy claim in a product pitch would imply.

### Gaps
- No independently verified, firm-sourced per-prediction directional-accuracy or hit-rate figure has ever been publicly disclosed by Renaissance Technologies, Two Sigma, Citadel, or D.E. Shaw. The only number in circulation (Medallion's ~50.75%) traces to a single secondhand anecdotal quote in a book, not to firm disclosure, an audit, court filing, or academic collaboration with the firm. Treat it as folklore/illustrative, not as verified fact.
- No court filings, academic collaborations, or leaked materials surfaced in this research that contain a hit-rate/accuracy number for any of the four named firms. It's possible such material exists but was not discoverable via the searches conducted (e.g., it may require access to litigation databases like PACER/Bloomberg Law, or specific SEC Form PF/ADV filings not indexed by general web search).

## What does Numerai publicly disclose about its models' real performance (correlation/accuracy metrics, what a good score looks like)?

### Takeaway
Numerai publicly documents its scoring methodology in detail (the "Numerai Corr" / CORR metric, Meta Model Contribution (MMC), Feature Neutral Correlation (FNC), True Contribution (TC)) but does **not** publish an official numeric definition of what counts as a "good" score — that benchmark exists only informally, via community/forum consensus, and is explicitly stated by a Numerai community member to have "no real good answer." Crucially, Numerai's own metric is a rank correlation between predictions and forward returns, not a binary up/down "accuracy" or "win rate" — so it is not directly comparable to a hit-rate percentage.

### Cited Findings
- Numerai's official scoring documentation defines CORR ("Numerai Corr") as a custom correlation variant: predictions are rank-transformed and Gaussianized, the target is mean-centered, then both series are raised to the 1.5 power (preserving sign) before taking the Pearson correlation — a method the docs say "accentuates the tails" because the fund trades its highest- and lowest-ranked predicted stocks most heavily. The docs explicitly state "you are only evaluated on your prediction's ranks," not the raw predicted values — [Numerai docs: Correlation (CORR)](https://docs.numer.ai/numerai-tournament/scoring/correlation-corr)
- Numerai offers multiple target/scoring variants: CORR20V2 (20-day target), CORR60, CORJ60, CORT20, plus Meta Model Contribution (MMC) and Feature Neutral Correlation (FNC) as additional/alternate leaderboard metrics; Numerai staff have stated in community office hours that MMC — not raw CORR — should arguably be the primary leaderboard metric going forward — [Numerai docs](https://docs.numer.ai/numerai-tournament/scoring/correlation-corr); [Office Hours recap](https://docs.numer.ai/community/community-built-products/numerai-community-office-hours/office-hours-recaps-season-2/ohwa-s02e08)
- A Numerai forum response to the direct question "what counts as a good correlation" states plainly: "there is no real good answer to that question" — [Numerai forum](https://forum.numer.ai/t/what-is-a-good-sharpe-ratio-and-validation-correlation/419)
- One forum poster estimated that ~0.468 is "probably about the highest numerai_corr score possible" (in-sample/theoretical ceiling, not a realistic live score) — [Numerai forum discussion](https://forum.numer.ai/t/submission-core-metrics/744)
- Community/informal benchmarks (not official Numerai documentation) suggest: random/noise targets produce correlation around 0.00 ± 0.01, while a real signal sits around 0.03 ± 0.02; for Numerai's older "Signals" product, a baseline CORR > 0.01 was considered a starting point, with well-tuned models plateauing around 0.03 — [Numerai forum thread on target discretization](https://forum.numer.ai/t/about-the-target-discretization/7081); [Japanese community write-up on Numerai Signals](https://zenn.dev/peter/articles/e4bbc8259a76a2)
- A third-party (non-Numerai, unverified) educational page categorizes CORR of roughly 0.02–0.05 as "Good," but this figure doesn't match any figure found in Numerai's own documentation, so it should be treated as unconfirmed/low-confidence — [OneBullEx academy page on Numerai](https://www.onebullex.com/academy/what-is-numerai-nmr-and-how-does-it-work)
- Payout/consistency is also tracked separately: one informal definition of "consistency" on the leaderboard is the fraction of eras (time periods) where a model's correlation exceeds 0.002 — [Numerai forum](https://forum.numer.ai/t/does-good-model-diagnostics-correlate-with-tournament-performance/1454)
- Numerai's crypto/staking payout formula is documented as `payout = stake_value × payout_factor × (corr × 1 + mmc × 2)`, capped at ±5% of stake per round; negative scores cause a portion of stake to be "burned" — [Numerai docs on crypto staking](https://docs.numer.ai/numerai-crypto/staking)

### Inferences
- Numerai deliberately does not use (and does not publish) a "directional accuracy" or binary hit-rate percentage as its headline metric — it uses a continuous rank-correlation measure instead, precisely because in real markets a usable, monetizable statistical edge is very small (single-digit percent correlation, not a binary "percent correct"). This is itself evidence supporting the broader finding that real quant performance is normally reported in correlation/Sharpe/returns terms rather than "accuracy %."
- Numerai's own community's inability to agree on a single "good score" number (despite years of leaderboard history) reinforces how different real, profitable quantitative signal strength looks from the intuitive "accuracy percentage" framing non-practitioners often expect.

### Gaps
- Numerai does not appear to publish an explicit official numeric threshold (e.g., "CORR of X is considered good") in its primary documentation; all specific numbers found (0.02, 0.03, 0.468 ceiling) come from community forum posts or third-party blogs, not from an official Numerai statement, and should be treated as informal consensus rather than disclosed fact.
- No official Numerai conversion was found translating its CORR/MMC scores into an equivalent "% directional accuracy," since the metric is a rank correlation rather than a classification accuracy; readers should not assume CORR ≈ accuracy %.

## Is there authoritative commentary on why quant funds don't publish per-prediction hit-rate numbers, and what win-rate range is realistic for profitable strategies even near a coin flip?

### Takeaway
Academic and industry research on hedge-fund disclosure broadly explains non-disclosure as a function of protecting proprietary edge and avoiding front-running, and finds that better-performing funds disclose *less* quantitative detail, not more. Separately, informal industry commentary (converging on the Medallion anecdote) consistently frames realistic profitable quant win-rates as only slightly above 50% (commonly cited informally as ~51–55%), with profitability coming from consistency, trade volume, and favorable risk/reward (expectancy) rather than high per-trade accuracy; trend-following systems are cited as sometimes profitable with win rates as low as ~40% because of asymmetric payoff size.

### Cited Findings
- An academic study of 3,000+ investor letters across 434 hedge funds found managers disclose a range of quantitative/qualitative info to investors, but that **better-performing funds disclose less quantitative data** about performance and holdings — consistent with a "proprietary cost" theory of disclosure (revealing details helps competitors free-ride on research) — [HU Berlin seminar paper (Cassar)](https://www.wiwi.hu-berlin.de/en/Professorships/bwl/finance/seminars/papers-fin-acc-res-seminars/16s/cassar.pdf)
- General finance-literature reasons funds avoid disclosure: (1) protecting proprietary trading ideas/edge from competitor free-riding; (2) avoiding front-running of illiquid positions; (3) fear that revealing a "speculative" bet in full reduces its chance of paying off — [Cambridge/JFQA: "Why Do Hedge Funds Avoid Disclosure? Evidence from Confidential 13F Filings"](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/why-do-hedge-funds-avoid-disclosure-evidence-from-confidential-13f-filings/D26B3BC0EF07E387B4E80519565C33E8); [EDHEC hedge fund reporting survey](https://climateimpact.edhec.edu/publications/edhec-hedge-fund-reporting-survey-2008)
- Hedge funds face materially lighter mandatory disclosure requirements than mutual funds under SEC rules, which do not require disclosure of detailed operational/holdings information — [HedgeFundAlpha summary of disclosure research](https://hedgefundalpha.com/news/why-hedge-funds-do-not-like-disclosing-positions/)
- Self-reported hedge fund performance data is also known to be unreliable: an Oxford/Duke study found that as many as 40% of funds revised previously reported performance figures — a further reason any voluntary accuracy claim from a fund should be treated skeptically even if one were offered — [HedgeCo.net coverage of Oxford/Duke study](https://www.hedgeco.net/news/?p=25859)
- Industry/forum commentary around the Medallion "50.75%" anecdote converges on the view that the figure illustrates "consistency + volume," not forecasting intelligence: a small, statistically reliable edge (just above 50%) compounded over a very large number of trades, combined with strict risk control, is what produces large aggregate profit — not a high per-trade "accuracy" in the way a classifier's accuracy is understood — [Goodreads reader notes on The Man Who Solved the Market](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/44698571-the-man-who-solved-the-market/58027759-thomas-wolf/800f9239-07c1-4032-a735-d1e2b9f74842)
- A related, frequently cited framing from trading-strategy discussion: trend-following systems are often profitable with win rates of 40% or lower, because expectancy (average win size × win rate minus average loss size × loss rate) — not raw win rate — determines profitability; a worked numeric example given in discussion: 4 wins at +6% and 6 losses at -2% nets +12% overall despite a 40% win rate — [TradingQnA forum discussion: "Is a 41% Accuracy Rate Really Highly Profitable?"](https://tradingqna.com/t/is-a-41-accuracy-rate-really-highly-profitable/194551)
- A related quote attributed to quant researcher Elwyn Berlekamp (cited in the context of the Renaissance book): "If you trade a lot, you only need to be right 51 percent of the time" — reinforcing the "slim edge + high volume" framing as a recognized quant-industry heuristic rather than one fund's unique secret — [Goodreads quotes page](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/68288870)

### Inferences
- The convergence of (a) academic disclosure research (better funds disclose less), (b) the sparse/anecdotal nature of even the most-cited number in the industry (Medallion's 50.75%, sourced to a secondhand remark, not a disclosure), and (c) the complete absence of any such figure for Two Sigma/Citadel/D.E. Shaw, together support a strong general conclusion: the normal, expected, and economically rational behavior for a successful quantitative fund is to publish aggregate returns (which investors require) while treating any granular hit-rate/accuracy statistic as confidential intellectual property. A tool, product, or pitch that claims a specific, precise stock-direction "accuracy percentage" (e.g., "73% accurate") should be treated with skepticism, since even the most successful, longest-running funds in the industry (by public returns) have never published such a number, and the one anecdote in circulation describes a win rate only barely above a coin flip (~50.75%) with profit coming from volume, consistency, and risk management, not from high directional accuracy.
- The "accuracy" framing itself may be somewhat of a misnomer for institutional quant practice: Numerai's own metric design (continuous rank correlation, not binary accuracy) and the "expectancy over win-rate" framing from trend-following commentary both suggest that sophisticated quant practice generally rejects "% directional accuracy" as the primary performance metric in favor of correlation, Sharpe ratio, and expectancy-based measures.

### Gaps
- No single authoritative academic paper or named finance professor was found that explicitly states a consensus numeric "realistic win-rate range" for profitable quant strategies (e.g., a stated "51-55% is realistic, below is unprofitable, above X is suspicious" claim from a cited academic source). The ranges mentioned above (51%, 50.75%, 40% for trend-following) come from anecdotes, forum discussion, and a single book-sourced quote rather than from a rigorous academic survey of strategy win-rates; this specific sourced number should be flagged as a gap if the report requires an authoritative, named-expert numeric range beyond the Medallion anecdote and the Berlekamp quote.
- A specific, on-point statement from Marcos López de Prado (a well-known quant academic/practitioner, author of *Advances in Financial Machine Learning*) directly addressing "accuracy near a coin flip but still profitable" was not found in this research; searches surfaced his related but distinct work on Sharpe ratio inference, backtest overfitting, and the Deflated Sharpe Ratio (e.g., with 1,000 backtests on a strategy with a true Sharpe of zero, the expected maximum observed Sharpe is ~3.3 purely from selection bias), which is relevant context on why *any* performance claim — accuracy or Sharpe — should be scrutinized for overfitting/selection bias, but is not a direct quote on realistic win-rates — [reference to his Deflated Sharpe Ratio work, cited secondhand via conference/LinkedIn summaries, not verified against the primary paper]
