# How the Quant/Finance Industry Measures and Talks About Prediction "Accuracy," Win Rate, and Error Rate

## What is the mathematically expected baseline/random-chance accuracy for binary (up/down) and 3-way (buy/sell/hold) stock direction prediction?

### Takeaway
A fair-coin binary up/down classifier has an expected baseline of 50% accuracy, and a balanced 3-way buy/sell/hold classifier has an expected baseline of 33.3%; but in real market data the "naive" (no-skill) baseline is often *harder to beat than pure chance* because of class imbalance (e.g., "hold"/"up" being the majority class) and because randomwalk-like price series are notoriously resistant to improvement over a naive persistence forecast.

### Cited Findings
- With three balanced classes, random guessing yields an expected accuracy of ~33.33%, since each observation has a 1/3 chance of being guessed correctly; a model must clear this bar to be considered better than chance — [cvms R package documentation, `baseline_multinomial`](https://rdrr.io/cran/cvms/man/baseline_multinomial.html)
- A 2024 arXiv paper on predicting market movements explicitly uses an equal-probability random decision (1/3 each) across buy/sell/hold as its baseline — [Combining supervised and unsupervised learning methods to predict financial market movements, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.03762)
- In practice, buy/sell/hold datasets are often class-imbalanced; a forum-documented test of a buy/hold/sell classifier scored 34–40% accuracy but it was unclear if this beat simply always predicting the majority class ("buy") — [Python Programming for Finance ML course community thread](https://www.pythonprogramming.net/community/454/Question%20on%20Python%20Programming%20for%20Finance%20Part%2012%20%28Machine%20Learning/)
- For binary directional forecasting, forecasting random walks is described as "notoriously challenging," with naïve (no-change / persistence) prediction serving as a difficult-to-beat baseline; the same source reports that once data leakage is removed, model accuracy often falls *below* that of naïve prediction — [arXiv walk-forward SPY benchmark paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26106)
- One paper cites directional accuracy figures in financial time series research ranging from 0.55 to 0.80, while a separate arXiv study found its own method only reliably exceeded a naive forecast with accuracies as low as 0.55 on S&P 500 and Bitcoin data — [arXiv, cited secondarily](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.14469v6)
- An experimental study published in the *Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control* found that relying on apparent patterns in past price data does not improve prediction accuracy, consistent with random-walk / efficient-market theory — [Predicting the unpredictable: new experimental evidence on forecasting random walks](https://nanyangtechnologicaluniv.demo.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/predicting-the-unpredictable-new-experimental-evidence-on-forecas)
- An older out-of-sample study of the Nikkei 225 found nearest-neighbour predictors performed worse than a random walk over the full test period — [cited in search results, title not independently re-verified]

### Inferences
- The "coin flip floor" for a binary up/down call is 50%; for a 3-way buy/sell/hold call with balanced classes it is 33.3%. These are the numbers any reported accuracy figure should be compared against before being called a "signal."
- However, the correct baseline in practice is not always the uniform-random one. If "hold" or "up" is the majority outcome in the test set (which is common — most single-day moves are small and many systems default to "hold"), the *majority-class* baseline can exceed 50%/33.3%, and a system must beat that higher bar, not just the naive coin-flip number, to claim real skill. This means any comparison across systems must specify (a) class balance in the test set and (b) whether the reported accuracy beat the majority-class baseline, not just a uniform random one.
- Because baseline accuracy depends heavily on class balance and the specific definition of "correct" (exact 3-way match vs. binary direction match vs. win-rate on executed trades only), comparing a system's raw "accuracy %" across systems without normalizing for the relevant baseline is close to meaningless.

### Gaps
- I did not find a single canonical textbook citation stating the 50%/33.3% baselines as a formal theorem for stock prediction specifically (it follows directly from basic probability for balanced classes); I've grounded it instead in applied ML documentation and empirical papers that explicitly use these baselines.
- I did not find a widely-cited academic consensus number for "typical no-skill baseline accuracy once class imbalance is accounted for" across the industry — this varies by dataset and would need to be computed per test set.

## Why do practitioners say win rate/accuracy alone is a weak or misleading signal of profitability? What is "payoff asymmetry" and why can a 35-45% win-rate system be profitable while a 60%+ win-rate system loses money?

### Takeaway
The number that determines profitability is **expectancy** — (win rate × average win size) − (loss rate × average loss size) — not win rate in isolation; because expectancy depends on the *ratio* of average win to average loss (the payoff/reward-to-risk ratio), a system only needs to clear a "breakeven win rate" of 1/(1+R) where R is the reward-to-risk ratio, meaning a 2:1 payoff system is profitable above ~33% win rate while a 1:3 payoff (small wins, occasional huge losses) system can lose money even above 60% win rate.

### Cited Findings
- Expectancy is defined as Expectancy = (Win Rate × Average Win) − (Loss Rate × Average Loss); this, not win rate, is "the number that actually matters" — [LuxAlgo, "Expectancy"](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/expectancy/)
- The breakeven win rate is 1/(1 + reward-to-risk ratio): 50% at a 1:1 payoff, ~33% at 2:1, and 25% at 3:1 (before fees/slippage) — [LuxAlgo, "Risk-Reward Ratio vs Win Rate"](https://www.luxalgo.com/blog/risk-reward-ratio-vs-win-rate-key-differences-2/)
- A 40% win rate with winners roughly twice the size of losers is profitable before costs, while a 75% win rate with rare but oversized losses can bleed money over time — [LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/blog/risk-reward-ratio-vs-win-rate-key-differences-2/); a similar point ("a 40% win rate can outperform a 70% win rate") is made independently by — [TradeZella, "Win Rate in Trading"](https://www.tradezella.com/blog/win-rate)
- Concrete real backtest example: a Turtle-style breakout system tested on EUR/USD had an average win of 1.25% and an average loss of 0.88%, giving a breakeven win rate of about 41%; the system actually won 40% of its trades and still lost money overall, with a profit factor of 0.94 — i.e., it fell *just* short of its own breakeven threshold despite a seemingly "coin flip-ish" win rate — [Backtrex, "Does the Turtle trading strategy work on EUR/USD?"](https://backtrex.com/en/backtests/turtle-trading-eur-usd)
- Trend-following systems structurally trade a low win rate for outsized winners (cutting losses quickly, letting winners run), while mean-reversion systems trade a high win rate for small, frequent gains punctuated by occasional large losses — [Daytradingtoolkit, "Trend Following"](https://daytradingtoolkit.com/dictionary/trend-following); turtletrader.com makes the same point under the heading "Winning Percentage Means Nothing: The Babe Ruth Lesson for Trend Followers" — [TurtleTrader.com](https://www.turtletrader.com/babe-ruth)
- An academic (physics-methods) paper on trend-following found that the P&L distribution of trend-following trades has an asymmetric, option-like payoff structure, and notes that roughly 40% winning trades is consistent with managers holding winning trades roughly twice as long as losing trades — a quantitative mechanism for why low win rate + long-run winners can still be profitable — [arXiv, "Trend followers lose more often than they gain"](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/physics/0508104)
- Stanley Druckenmiller has said the most important lesson he learned from George Soros was that performance is about "how much you make when you're right and how much you lose when you're wrong" — not whether you are right or wrong — [25iq.com, "A Dozen Things I've Learned from Stanley Druckenmiller"](https://25iq.com/2015/04/11/a-dozen-things-ive-learned-from-stanley-druckenmiller-about-investing/); a secondary source paraphrases this as "the key is not whether one is right or wrong, but how much one makes when right and how much one loses when wrong" (this framing of the quote is corroborated across multiple secondary sources, but I could not locate Druckenmiller's or Soros's own primary-source wording, so treat the exact phrasing as secondhand)
- A separate claim that "Soros made money on fewer than 30% of his trades" appears in at least one source but is explicitly a secondhand/unverified claim, not a documented statistic — [Daily Speculations](https://dailyspeculations.com/wordpress/?p=13988) (flagged as low-confidence)
- A 60% win rate with poor payoff sizing can still fail a formal statistical significance test on mean returns (i.e., still be statistically indistinguishable from a zero-edge strategy) — [LuxAlgo, "Significance of Returns"](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/significance-of-returns.md)

### Inferences
- The core reason win rate alone is misleading is that it collapses a two-dimensional problem (frequency of being right × magnitude when right/wrong) into one number. Two systems can report identical "accuracy" (e.g., both 45%) and have opposite profitability if their payoff ratios differ — one could have 2:1 average win:loss (profitable, expectancy positive) and the other 1:2 (unprofitable, expectancy negative).
- This directly implies that when comparing stock-prediction systems that report different metrics (3-way exact match, binary direction match, win rate on executed trades, Sharpe ratio, risk-adjusted return), the *only* fair comparison is one that also accounts for the magnitude/payoff dimension — e.g., expectancy, profit factor, or Sharpe ratio — not raw hit-rate percentages side by side.
- A system reporting "accuracy" on a prediction task (e.g., exact match to a labeled buy/sell/hold target) is answering a different question than a system reporting "win rate on executed trades" — the latter already reflects a payoff-asymmetric filter (trades were only taken/counted when some threshold was met), so the two numbers are not interchangeable even if both are expressed as percentages.

### Gaps
- I could not find a primary-source (Soros's or Druckenmiller's own written/interview) exact quote with precise wording or a documented win-rate percentage for Soros's own trading; the specific "30%" figure should be treated as folklore rather than verified data.
- I did not find an additional second named, verifiable real-world fund/trader case study beyond the Druckenmiller/Soros anecdote and the Turtle-system backtest; the Turtle backtest example (Backtrex) is a third-party reconstruction, not primary performance data from the original 1983 Turtles.

## What statistical significance / sample-size considerations apply when comparing accuracy claims from a few dozen/hundred test cases vs. thousands of trades?

### Takeaway
A reported accuracy figure is only as trustworthy as the confidence interval around it, and that interval shrinks with the square root of sample size; practitioner sources converge on "a few hundred trades across more than one market regime" as a rough minimum before treating a win rate as a property of the strategy rather than of the sample, and tens of trades (e.g., n=20-30) are widely considered insufficient to distinguish a claimed edge from pure chance.

### Cited Findings
- The Wilson score interval is recommended over the naive normal-approximation interval ("rate ± z × standard error") for small samples or extreme proportions, because the naive interval can collapse or overshoot (e.g., imply impossible values like >100% or <0%) in those regimes — [quantum-algo.com glossary, "Wilson Score Interval"](https://www.quantum-algo.com/glossary/wilson-score-interval/)
- The Agresti–Coull interval is a documented alternative with "markedly better coverage properties, particularly for small sample sizes or when the observed proportion is close to 0 or 1" — [cited academic source via search results]
- Margin of error for a binomial proportion decreases as sample size increases; one trading-focused guide gives the standard approximation margin ≈ 1.96 × √(p(1−p)/n) and notes doubling the sample size reduces the margin by roughly 30% (consistent with the 1/√2 ≈ 0.71 scaling factor) — [traderssecondbrain.com, "How many trades to know if a strategy works"](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/how-many-trades-to-know-if-strategy-works)
- Most quantitative practitioners want to see a win rate measured over "a few hundred trades spanning more than one [market] regime" before treating it as a stable property of the strategy rather than an artifact of the specific sample — [LuxAlgo, "Win Rate"](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/win-rate/)
- A concrete illustrative example from a trading-tools vendor: a 65% win rate measured over 20 trades is explicitly described as NOT statistically significant, while the same 65% win rate measured over 200 trades IS statistically significant; the same source recommends 100+ trades for a reliable Monte Carlo-based simulation and explicitly tests win rate against a 50% coin-flip null — [Aeromir, "Monte Carlo Simulator Help"](https://futures.aeromir.com/montecarlohelp)
- There is no single universal minimum trade count; sufficiency depends on the decision being made, the shape of the trade return distribution, and the diversity of market conditions sampled; the same source states that twenty trades are "rarely enough" to support a strong conclusion — [MQL5, "How Much Data Is Enough to Trust a Trading Strategy?"](https://www.mql5.com/en/blogs/post/775592)
- A confidence interval on a win rate assumes independent trades drawn from a stable (stationary) underlying process; real trading edges can drift over time and real trades can be serially correlated/clustered, which violates that assumption and makes textbook confidence intervals optimistic — [traderssecondbrain.com](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/how-many-trades-to-know-if-strategy-works)
- A statistically well-established past win rate is still not a guarantee of a future win rate — [traderssecondbrain.com](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/how-many-trades-to-know-if-strategy-works)
- A win-rate confidence interval by itself doesn't establish profitability; the more relevant test is whether the *lower bound* of the confidence interval clears the breakeven win rate implied by the strategy's reward-to-risk ratio (not just whether it clears 50%) — [traderssecondbrain.com](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/how-many-trades-to-know-if-strategy-works)

### Inferences (calculations below are my own application of the standard normal-approximation binomial confidence interval formula, margin ≈ 1.96·√(p̂(1−p̂)/n), using the methodology corroborated by the cited sources above — not a quoted figure from any single source)
- At n=30 test cases: a reported 55% "accuracy" has an approximate 95% CI of roughly 37%–73% — this is statistically indistinguishable from a 50% coin flip. A reported 85% accuracy has an approximate CI of roughly 72%–98% — distinguishable from chance, but the interval is still wide enough that the "true" rate could plausibly be anywhere from borderline-good to near-perfect. A reported 35% accuracy has a CI of roughly 18%–52%, which (on a simple 2-way "beat the coin flip" test) cannot be confidently called worse than chance either.
- At n=100: a 55% accuracy CI narrows to roughly 45%–65% (still overlapping 50% — not yet distinguishable from chance on a binary task). An 85% accuracy CI narrows to roughly 78%–92%. A 35% accuracy CI narrows to roughly 26%–44%.
- At n=1,000: a 55% accuracy CI narrows to roughly 52%–58% (now clearly distinguishable from a 50% coin flip). An 85% accuracy CI narrows to roughly 83%–87%. A 35% accuracy CI narrows to roughly 32%–38%.
- Practical implication for comparing systems: a "35% exact 3-way match accuracy" figure from a few dozen test cases is not necessarily worse than a "55% binary direction accuracy" figure from a few dozen cases once the respective baselines (33.3% vs 50%) and sample-size uncertainty are both accounted for — both could be statistically indistinguishable from their own chance baselines. Conversely, a "55%" figure becomes a genuinely meaningful, hard-to-dismiss signal only once it is backed by hundreds to low-thousands of independent test cases. This is the crux of why cross-system accuracy comparisons are unreliable without disclosing both the baseline and the sample size.
- A defensible floor, synthesizing the cited guidance: treat any accuracy/win-rate claim based on fewer than ~100 independent trades/predictions as provisional at best (consistent with the Aeromir and MQL5 sources both flagging ~20 trades as insufficient); treat "a few hundred spanning more than one regime" (per LuxAlgo) as the threshold where a claimed edge starts to be taken seriously; and recognize that even thousands of trades drawn from a single, unusually calm regime (as in the Quantopian study below, which spans largely one bull market) may still not generalize.

### Gaps
- I did not find one single, universally-cited "defensible minimum sample size" number endorsed by a top-tier academic source (e.g., a specific n from López de Prado's work prescribing a minimum trade count); the "few hundred trades" and "100+" figures come from applied/vendor practitioner sources rather than a peer-reviewed statistical paper specific to trading. The confidence-interval mathematics itself (Wilson score, normal approximation) is standard statistics, independently well-established, and not in dispute.
- The real-world independence/stationarity caveat (trades are not i.i.d.) means textbook sample-size thresholds are a lower bound on the evidence needed, not a sufficient condition — I found this point stated qualitatively but no sourced method for how much larger a sample needs to be to compensate for serial correlation in this specific domain.

## Are there documented examples of stock-prediction systems/papers that reported high accuracy (70%+) but used flawed methodology (data leakage, improper splits, survivorship bias, in-sample testing)?

### Takeaway
Yes — this is a well-documented, named problem in both quantitative finance generally (backtest overfitting) and specifically in financial machine learning and, most recently, in LLM-based financial prediction agents; multiple peer-reviewed and widely-cited sources document concrete mechanisms and before/after numbers showing how performance claims can be artifacts of methodology rather than real predictive skill.

### Cited Findings
- **Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu (2014), "Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial Charlatanism: The Effects of Backtest Overfitting on Out-of-Sample Performance,"** *Notices of the American Mathematical Society*, Vol. 61, No. 5, pp. 458–471: the paper argues that many financial strategies and fund designs supported by published "backtests" were in fact statistical-overfitting artifacts; it demonstrates with online tools that a strategy can show an in-sample Sharpe ratio of 1.59 while its true out-of-sample Sharpe ratio is -0.18 — i.e., a strategy that looks like a strong, reliable performer in testing is actually a loser live — [summarized via improbable.com / mathinvestor.org coverage](https://mathinvestor.org/?p=1571); the paper's own recommendation is that no more than ~45 variations of a strategy should be tested on a given dataset before the result should be presumed overfit — [per CXO Advisory / Bailey et al. summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/backtest-overfitting-the-movies/). This paper received mainstream press coverage; the *Financial Times* described the authors as arguing that "the vast majority of claims being made for quantitative investment strategies are false" — [Financial Times, cited via jonborwein.org coverage](https://jonborwein.org/2016/08/jon-left-this-world-a-better-place/)
- **Bailey & López de Prado (2014), "The Deflated Sharpe Ratio: Correcting for Selection Bias, Backtest Overfitting and Non-Normality,"** published in the *Journal of Portfolio Management* — a companion statistical tool specifically designed to correct backtested Sharpe ratios for the fact that researchers typically report only the best of many tried variants (selection bias under multiple testing) — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551)
- **López de Prado is directly quoted** (via university course slides) as saying "Most backtests published in journals are flawed, as the result of selection bias on multiple tests" — [Palomar, HKUST lecture slides on backtesting portfolios](https://palomar.home.ece.ust.hk/MAFS5310_lectures/slides_backtesting.pdf)
- **Wiecki, Campbell, Lent & Stauth, "All That Glitters Is Not Gold: Comparing Backtest and Out-of-Sample Performance on a Large Cohort of Trading Algorithms"** (Quantopian, 2016): examined 888 real user-submitted equity trading algorithms with at least 6–12 months of true out-of-sample (live/paper) performance (2010–2015). Key findings: in-sample Sharpe ratio explained essentially none of out-of-sample performance (R² < 0.025); across backtest metrics generally, predictability of OOS performance was low (R² < 0.25); critically, **the more a user iterated on/backtested a given algorithm, the larger the gap between its in-sample and out-of-sample performance** — a direct, measured signature of overfitting via repeated testing; a machine-learning model using multiple backtest-derived features reached R² = 0.17 on held-out data and a portfolio built from its picks achieved an out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of 1.2, "significantly better" than a portfolio built by simply picking the algorithms with the highest backtest Sharpe ratios — [Quantpedia summary of the paper](https://quantpedia.com/quantopians-academic-paper-about-in-vs-out-of-sample-performance-of-trading-alg/); caveat noted independently: the aggregate in-sample/out-of-sample window was fairly short and occurred mostly within a single low-volatility bull market, limiting how far the conclusions generalize — [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/in-sample-vs-out-of-sample-performance-of-888-trading-strategies)
- **Zachary David (2019), "Information leakage in financial machine learning research,"** *Algorithmic Finance*, Vol. 8, Issues 1–2, pp. 1–4 (editorial opening a themed issue): reports that **more than half of submissions** to financial-ML research venues — and some published papers too — contain errors caused by information leakage; documents specific recurring mechanisms: (1) survivorship bias from universe selection — "selecting a set of companies which currently exist means the algorithm ignores those that have merged or gone bankrupt over the sample period"; (2) look-ahead/feature-target leakage — e.g., a feature set built from the same closing prices used to define the prediction target, letting the model effectively see the answer; (3) normalizing/scaling data over the full sample (including future/test data) before splitting. The editorial also states that PLoS ONE opened investigations into at least two papers in this space over reproducibility/implausible-performance concerns — [SAGE Journals, Algorithmic Finance](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.3233/AF-190900); confirmed independently via search-result excerpts since full-text access returned HTTP 403
- A 2024 survey of reinforcement-learning-in-finance papers found that some apply their methodology only to successful, currently-listed single stocks (e.g., Apple), neglecting to test on stocks that have since gone bankrupt or been delisted — a textbook survivorship-bias flaw — [arXiv, "The Evolution of Reinforcement Learning in Quantitative Finance: A Survey"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.10932)
- **Most directly relevant to LLM-based stock prediction agents** (closely analogous to Gemini-based prediction systems): **"Profit Mirage: Revisiting Information Leakage in LLM-based Financial Agents"** (arXiv:2510.07920, October 2025) documents a "profit mirage" phenomenon directly: LLM-based trading agents (GPT-4o-based, training cutoff October 2023) showed strong backtested returns, but once evaluated on a post-cutoff period the best-performing agent's return decayed by roughly 50%, which the authors describe as becoming "a statistical zero" — i.e., the apparent skill evaporates once the model can no longer be implicitly recalling memorized post-hoc market outcomes from its pretraining data rather than genuinely forecasting. Specific decay figures across tested agents: Total Return decay ranged from 50.18% (TradingAgents) to 71.85% (FinMem); Sharpe Ratio decay ranged from 51.48% (QuantAgent) to 62.23% (FinCON), with TradingAgents at 55.68%; this occurred despite the pre-cutoff test period (Q2–Q3 2021, +13.79% market return) and post-cutoff test period (Q3–Q4 2024, +13.35% market return) having very similar overall market conditions, ruling out "it was just a different/harder regime" as the explanation. The paper also ran a memorization audit (FinLeak-Bench) finding the underlying LLMs could answer historical-market-fact questions (e.g., "what was the price/trend on date X") with 85–93% accuracy purely from memorized pretraining knowledge, not genuine forecasting; and ran a fine-tuning test showing in-distribution accuracy jumping from 51.61% to 72.16% (Qwen2.5-7B) and 54.73% to 76.52% (Llama-3.1-8B) after fine-tuning, while true out-of-distribution forecasting accuracy on genuinely unseen data *fell* by 21.53 and 18.06 percentage points respectively — a clean, quantified demonstration that higher reported accuracy from fine-tuning/retrieval can come entirely from memorization rather than predictive skill — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07920) and [full HTML text](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07920v1)

### Inferences
- Across both classical quant backtesting (Bailey/López de Prado, Quantopian/888-algorithm study) and the newest LLM-agent literature (Profit Mirage), the pattern is structurally identical: a system is tested on data that overlaps, directly or indirectly, with information available at "training"/"fitting" time (whether via repeated backtesting on the same historical window, via scaling/splitting errors, via survivorship-biased universes, or via an LLM's pretraining corpus containing the actual historical outcomes), and the resulting accuracy/Sharpe/return figure collapses — sometimes to a "statistical zero" — once genuinely out-of-sample, post-cutoff, or counterfactual data is used.
- The Profit Mirage paper is especially load-bearing for any project built on an LLM (e.g., Gemini) making stock predictions: it shows concretely that an LLM-based prediction agent can score very well on historical/backtest-style evaluation precisely because the underlying model may already "know" (from pretraining) how the historical period actually played out, and that this is empirically distinguishable from genuine forecasting skill via a pre-cutoff vs. post-cutoff split. Any accuracy claim for an LLM-based stock predictor should disclose whether its test period is entirely after the underlying model's training/knowledge cutoff, and ideally report the same metric on both sides of that cutoff to check for the "decay" signature documented in this paper.
- The "high accuracy that turns out to be overfitting/leakage" pattern is not a fringe or occasional problem — the Zachary David editorial's "more than half of submissions" figure and the Quantopian study's finding that *more* backtesting literally *predicts* a *larger* in-sample/out-of-sample gap both suggest this is close to the norm rather than the exception in published/submitted financial ML work, which argues for treating any single-digit-sourced 70%+ accuracy claim with structural skepticism until the test methodology (point-in-time universe, true chronological holdout, no re-use of test data for tuning, no model pretraining-data overlap) is explicitly disclosed.

### Gaps
- I did not find a named, public "post-mortem" of a *commercial/branded* stock-prediction product (as opposed to an academic paper or open research platform like Quantopian) being exposed for 70%+ accuracy claims built on flawed methodology — the strongest, most concrete, numbers-backed examples I found are academic/research (Bailey et al.'s overfitting-tool demonstration, the Quantopian 888-algorithm study, and the Profit Mirage LLM-agent paper). If the report needs a specific commercial-product scandal, that would require a separate targeted search outside this scope (and the task constraints explicitly steer away from naming specific commercial products).
- The "half of submissions have leakage" statistic from Zachary David (2019) is the author's own editorial claim introducing a themed journal issue; I could not independently cross-verify this percentage against a second source, and I could not access the full text directly (SAGE returned HTTP 403), so this is reported via corroborating search-result excerpts rather than a direct full-text read.
- The Profit Mirage paper's four "dimensions" of leakage are: (1) backtesting vs. generalization (temporal/cutoff split), (2) counterfactual evaluation (perturbed inputs), (3) memorization audits (FinLeak-Bench QA), and (4) before/after fine-tuning comparison — I was able to confirm these four categories and representative numbers for each, but did not extract every per-agent table value (e.g., full counterfactual leakage scores for all tested agents, only representative highs/lows for FinMem and TradingAgents).
